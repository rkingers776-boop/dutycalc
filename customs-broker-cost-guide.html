Do You Need a Customs Broker? The Law Says No. The Math Is More Interesting.

The first broker quote most importers ever see is a line item on a freight invoice: $145 for the entry, $65 for the ISF, and something called a disbursement fee that nobody explains because nobody wants to. It looks like a rounding error next to the ocean freight, which is exactly why most people approve it without reading the rest of the page.

The second quote is the one that matters, and it doesn't come from the broker. It comes from CBP, usually months later, after an entry has been liquidated and someone notices that a commodity code was two digits off the product that actually arrived. At that point a $145 filing fee is not the topic of conversation.

So the question is worth answering honestly, and the honest answer is that no US law requires you to hire a customs broker. You can file your own entries. The reason most importers don't has nothing to do with legality and everything to do with arithmetic — and that arithmetic flips at a fairly specific volume.

Short version
  • Self-filing is legal. What it requires is a filer code, an ACE Portal account, ACE-certified filing software, a bond, a payment route and five years of records.
  • A formal ocean entry runs $125–$175 in broker fees. A full container entry lands at roughly $400–$700 once ISF, bond and CBP's processing fee are counted.
  • The disbursement fee — 2–3% of the duty the broker advances — is the line that grows with your duty bill. An ACH account with CBP removes it entirely.
  • The fee is never the expensive part. Classification errors and missed deadlines are.

The law is not the obstacle. The filing system is.

CBP's position is straightforward: the importer of record is responsible for the entry, and the importer of record may file it. There is no licence requirement on your side of the desk. If you want to be your own broker in the sense that matters — filing the paperwork and paying the duty — you are allowed to.

What you are not allowed to do is file on paper at your convenience. ACE has been mandatory for US imports since 2016, so a self-filer needs:

What self-filing actually requires
  • A filer code, obtained from CBP, so entries can be filed under your own identity.
  • An ACE Secure Data Portal account. CBP charges nothing for portal access, and the account itself takes around twenty minutes to create.
  • ACE-certified filing software or a filing service, because nobody keys multi-line entries into a web form by hand at volume.
  • A customs bond on file — single-entry or continuous — before the first commercial entry.
  • A payment route: either your own ACH account with CBP, or a broker who advances the duty and bills you for the privilege.
  • Five years of entry records, retrievable on request.

Look at that list again and notice what is missing: the licence. What self-filing actually costs you is software and standing obligations. The portal is free; the plumbing around it is not, and it never stops needing attention. That is the honest reason the "just do it yourself" advice falls apart somewhere around your sixth or seventh entry of the year. If your goods are simple, your lines are few and your classification is not in doubt, the plumbing is manageable. If any of those three conditions is false, you are buying expertise whether or not you hire anyone — you are just buying it in your own evenings.

One number is worth holding onto here. CBP's own regulatory analyses value importer time at $36.57 per hour in loaded labour cost. That is the government's estimate of what an hour of your compliance work is worth. It is not a generous number, and it is the yardstick to use when you compare a $145 entry fee against doing the work yourself.

What actually shows up on a broker's invoice

Broker pricing is less mysterious than it looks, because almost everyone in the industry prices the same components. The variation is in how many of them appear on your quote and how many appear later as a surprise.

Line item Typical 2026 range What it actually covers
Formal entry — ocean $125–$175 Classification, entry preparation and electronic filing with CBP.
Formal entry — air $95–$150 Same work, lower document volume, faster release cycle.
Informal entry (goods under $2,500) $60–$95 Reduced-data filing for low-value commercial shipments.
ISF filing (10+2) $50–$85 Advance manifest data, due 24 hours before vessel loading.
Additional HTS lines $3–$10 each Charged per line beyond the three to five usually bundled in.
Partner government agency handling $15–$50 per agency FDA, EPA, USDA, DOT or FCC coordination on regulated goods.
Duty disbursement fee 2–3% of duty advanced A financing charge on duty the broker pays CBP before you reimburse it.
Single-entry bond $75–$275 Per-shipment bond, sized to the entry's duty and fees.
All-in, one ocean container entry $400–$700 Broker fee, ISF, bond and CBP's merchandise processing fee together.

Two of those lines do not belong to the broker at all. The merchandise processing fee is CBP's own charge, assessed at 0.3464% of the entered value, with a floor of $31.67 and a ceiling of $614.35 per entry. The bond is underwritten by a surety, not the broker, even when the broker arranges it. If you want to see how those pieces land on a total, the landed cost calculator itemises duty, VAT and fees side by side, and the customs bond calculator works through the single-entry versus continuous decision on its own because it deserves its own page.

Ask for the fee schedule in writing before the first shipment, itemised, with the disbursement percentage stated as a number. A broker who will not put it in writing before you commit is telling you something about what happens after you commit.

The disbursement fee is the line that scales with your duty bill

Everything else on that table is roughly fixed per entry. The disbursement fee is not, and that is the whole problem with it.

Here is the mechanism. Duty is payable to CBP at the time of entry. If you have your own ACH account on file, the money moves from your bank account and that is the end of it. If you do not, the broker pays CBP and invoices you, often on terms of ten to thirty days. In exchange for carrying that float, the broker adds 2 to 3 percent of the duty amount. It is a financing charge dressed as an administrative one, and it grows with the size of the duty bill rather than the size of the work.

Run the arithmetic on a mid-sized importer. Duty of $8,000 a month at a 2.5 percent disbursement rate is $200 a month, or $2,400 a year, paid for the convenience of not filing a bank form. Set up an ACH account with CBP and that line goes to zero. You keep the broker for classification and filing, which is what you were actually buying, and you stop renting their balance sheet.

The 2026 detail that makes this worse: duty bills are larger than they were. Section 301 layers on Chinese-origin goods, sector tariffs on metals and vehicles, and the suspension of the $800 de minimis exemption in June 2026 that pushed a large volume of low-value commercial shipments out of the duty-free lane all raise the duty line. A percentage of that line quietly rose with it, without anyone renegotiating the fee schedule.

This is also where the fee conversation should happen. Broker fees are negotiable at volume; the disbursement rate is usually the easiest thing to remove because removing it costs the broker nothing but a form. Ask for ACH, then ask whether the rate on the rest of the schedule moves once you are past a hundred entries a year.

The break-even, honestly

Here is where self-filing genuinely pays, and where it stops paying. The variable that matters is not how large your shipments are — it is how many formal entries you file and how much doubt surrounds your commodity codes.

Formal entries a year Self-filing reality What we would actually do
1–5 Two or three clean lines, unregulated goods, no origin complications. Setup is an afternoon of ACE and bond paperwork, and there is no software bill worth amortising. Self-filing is viable. Budget the setup hours and keep every entry record from day one.
6–30 The software layer starts to matter, and so does the cost of your own evening hours at CBP's $36.57 benchmark. One classification error per year wipes out the savings. Use a broker. Negotiate the schedule, insist on ACH, and treat the fee as cheap insurance.
31–150 Filing is now a recurring process with deadlines, bond balances and PGA coordination attached to it. The infrastructure is a part-time job. Use a broker with commodity expertise in your product category. Audit the disbursement line annually.
150+ At this volume the question shifts from who files to whether you need in-house trade compliance capability. Broker plus an internal owner of classification. That is a different decision from the one on this page.

Notice that nowhere in that table did the size of the fee decide anything. At five entries a year the entire difference between filing yourself and paying a broker is a few hundred dollars — less than a single afternoon of properly valued compliance work. At thirty entries it is a few thousand. Neither number is what should keep an importer awake.

When it goes wrong, it is almost never the fee

The failure modes in customs are not spread evenly across the process. They cluster around two things: what you declared, and when you declared it.

Misclassification is the first. A code that is plausible but wrong produces the wrong duty rate, and if the product sits inside an active antidumping or countervailing order, it produces a liability that has nothing to do with the normal tariff schedule. Our AD/CVD guide walks through how cash deposits work and why the final rate can move against you after the fact; the HS code guide covers the classification process itself. This is the expertise you are actually paying for when you hire a broker. Classification judgement is the product. Filing is the delivery mechanism.

The second is deadline failure. An ISF for ocean freight is due 24 hours before the vessel loads at the foreign port. Miss that and it is not a paperwork problem, it is a penalty problem, with late or incomplete filings carrying exposure of up to $5,000 per violation. Understating or misdeclaring value brings its own exposure under 19 USC §1592. A customs hold that follows from either can leave you with storage and demurrage on top, which our customs hold guide covers in operational detail.

And there is a records problem that most first-time importers do not discover until someone asks. Entries have to be retained for five years, and the obligation belongs to the importer of record regardless of who filed. If you used a third-party importer of record service to avoid the whole business, the liability did not transfer with the paperwork — that is the specific trap described in our importer of record guide.

The asymmetry to remember
  • The broker's fee is a known number you agree to in advance.
  • The cost of a wrong commodity code is an unknown number that arrives later, with interest and a lawyer attached.
  • Self-filing saves you the first and exposes you to the second. That trade is only rational when classification is genuinely trivial.

How to vet one in ten minutes

Most of the risk in hiring a broker is concentrated in the first conversation, and it is answerable with three questions and one lookup.

Ask What a good answer sounds like Walk away if
Are you on CBP's permitted broker list, and what is your filer code? A filer code offered without hesitation, and an entity name that matches the licence entry. Hedging, or a filer code belonging to a different company than the one signing your power of attorney.
Send me your itemised fee schedule. A written schedule separating entry, ISF, extra lines, PGA handling, disbursement percentage, exam and storage. Anyone who quotes a single per-shipment number and calls the rest "standard".
What is your disbursement percentage, and can I file ACH instead? A straight answer, and a willingness to set you up on ACH. Resistance to ACH, because it is the broker's cheapest cost to remove.
Have you classified my product category before? Specific codes, specific rulings, specific agencies involved in your goods. General importer experience offered as a substitute for your commodity type.
Can I see entries filed in my name in ACE? An offer to walk you through your own ACE Portal access from the start. Reluctance to give you visibility into your own filings.

One piece of context that reframes the licence question: CBP's annual permit fee for a licensed broker is $185.38. That is the whole price of the credential. It is not what you are buying, and a broker who leads with licensing is answering a question you were not asking. Verify the licence because it is binary and cheap to check — then spend the rest of the conversation on commodity experience and the fee schedule, which is where the money actually is.

Two supporting documents make this conversation concrete rather than theoretical. Before you request quotes, run your scenario through the import duty calculator so you know what duty should be, and through the de minimis checker to confirm whether you are even in a formal entry lane after the 2026 changes. Importers who arrive with a duty number in hand get quoted differently than importers who arrive with a question mark.

The short answer

If you file two or three formal entries a year, your goods are not regulated by an agency, your commodity codes are not in dispute and you are comfortable working inside ACE, self-filing is a reasonable choice. Everything you need is free at the portal, and the plumbing is a weekend project.

If you file six or more, or your goods touch FDA, EPA or USDA jurisdiction, or your origin is China and Section 301 layers apply, or you cannot state your HTS code from memory, hire a licensed broker. Then do the three things that make the relationship cost less: verify the licence and the filer code, get the fee schedule in writing, and put yourself on ACH so you stop paying 2 to 3 percent on every duty bill.

The decision rule that survives contact with real shipments is narrower than "hire an advisor" and wider than "save the fee". Buy the classification, not the filing. A broker who only files forms is an expensive courier. A broker who catches one bad commodity code before it reaches CBP is the cheapest line item in your cost structure, and the fee argument evaporates the first time that happens.

Wherever you land, the numbers should be yours before someone else quotes them to you. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I legally need a customs broker to import into the US?

No. CBP allows the importer of record to file its own entries, and there is no licence requirement on the importer's side. What self-filing requires is operational: a filer code, an ACE Portal account, ACE-certified filing software or a filing service, a customs bond, a payment route and five years of retained records.

How much does a customs broker charge per entry in 2026?

A formal ocean entry runs $125 to $175 and a formal air entry $95 to $150, with informal entries at $60 to $95. ISF filing adds $50 to $85, extra HTS lines $3 to $10 each beyond those included, and partner agency handling $15 to $50 per agency. Count the bond and CBP's merchandise processing fee and a standard ocean container entry lands at roughly $400 to $700.

What is a disbursement fee, and can I avoid it?

It is the 2 to 3 percent a broker charges for advancing duty to CBP and billing you later. Because it is calculated on the duty amount, it grows as duty grows — at $8,000 a month in duty, a 2.5 percent rate costs about $2,400 a year. Setting up an ACH account with CBP makes the charge disappear without changing anything else about the relationship.

Can a freight forwarder be my customs broker?

Only if it holds its own CBP customs broker licence. Many forwarders operate an in-house brokerage, but the licence, liability and fee schedule are distinct from the freight service, and you can appoint an independent broker to work with any forwarder. Check which legal entity signs the power of attorney.

How do I verify that a broker is licensed?

Look it up on CBP's permitted customs brokers list, searchable by port, which shows brokers holding an active licence and national permit. If the company is not on it, filings are happening under another company's licence. Then confirm the filer code matches the entity named in your power of attorney.

Is self-filing actually cheaper?

At very low volume with simple, unregulated goods, yes. Above a handful of formal entries a year, usually no. ACE Portal access is free, but filing at volume needs certified software, and CBP's own analysis prices importer time at $36.57 an hour. Add bond administration and the risk-adjusted cost of one classification error and the per-entry broker fee becomes the cheaper line.
