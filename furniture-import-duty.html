Furniture Import Duty, Without the Hand-Waving

Everyone quotes furniture at "about 8%." That number is a distraction. After Section 301 it's 15.5% — and if you're importing wooden bedroom sets from the wrong Chinese factory, it can be 15.5% plus an anti-dumping deposit of up to 216%. Here's what actually decides your bill.

Before you trust any rate
Every percentage below is a typical figure for a common HTS subheading. Furniture classification turns on material (wood/metal/rattan), function (seat vs. storage), and whether the piece is wooden bedroom furniture under the anti-dumping order. The only number that binds you is the 10-digit code on your commercial invoice. Our HS Code Duty Estimator gives a baseline; a furniture broker gives certainty.

Why "8% furniture duty" is the wrong starting point

The base MFN rate across Chapter 94 averages around 8%, with subheadings swinging from 0% to 12%. Metal chairs and seats come in low (0–5.5%); wooden storage and beds sit higher (8–10%); rattan and bamboo can hit 12%. That's the number people quote. But for Chinese furniture it's not the number you pay.

On top of MFN, Chinese furniture carries Section 301 List 3 at 7.5%, stacking on the same CIF base. So most pieces land at 15.5% combined — not 8%. And that's before you even reach the anti-dumping landmine below. The 8% figure survives because it's what the HTS book says; it is not what CBP collects.

Chapter 94 — where furniture lives

  • 9401 — Seats and chairs. Dining chairs, office chairs, sofas, recliners. Upholstered wooden-frame seating (9401.61) is the workhorse code. MFN 0% to 5.5% — lower than most furniture, but upholstery draws more CBP attention because of fabric and filling complexity.
  • 9403 — Other furniture. Desks, tables, cabinets, beds, shelves. This is the big one. 9403.50 = wooden bedroom furniture (the AD-exposed category); 9403.30 = wooden office; 9403.60 = other wooden. MFN 0% to 12%, most wooden pieces 8–10%. Metal under this heading often runs 3–5%.
  • 9404 — Mattresses and bedding. Innerspring units can be free; foam mattresses 3–6%. Mattresses also face separate flammability rules (16 CFR Part 1633) enforced at the border.

Material flips the code: a desk that's mostly metal with a wood top can classify as 9403.20 (metal) at a lower rate than the wood-frame version at 9403.60. CBP uses "essential character" to decide. If you have flexibility on frame material, ask your broker to run it both ways — on a full container the difference is real money.

Duty by furniture type — and the anti-dumping landmine

This is the section you can't afford to skim. Chinese wooden bedroom furniture has been under a US anti-dumping order — case A-570-890 — since 2005. The deposit rates are brutal and they're set per factory: from 0% (cooperative producers who proved market pricing) to 216% (the China-wide rate for any factory that didn't participate). For the full mechanics of AD/CVD orders and how they stack on MFN and Section 301, see our Anti-Dumping & CVD guide.

Two hundred sixteen percent is not a typo. A $10,000 container of beds can generate a $21,600 deposit before a dollar of regular duty or Section 301 even hits. The rate isn't fixed — Commerce runs annual reviews, and a factory at 0% three years ago can be 41% today if it stopped responding to questionnaires. Before you place a single PO for wooden bedroom furniture, look up the manufacturer's AD rate in the ITA ACCESS database (case A-570-890). If the factory can't give you its producer case number, walk away. The order covers dressers, beds, nightstands, armoires, chests — not kitchen cabinets, office desks, or living-room shelving, but CBP scrutinizes anything that could be called bedroom.

The order is a deposit, not a final bill: Commerce's annual review can adjust the rate retroactively, so you might pay 41% now and get billed for another 20% (or refunded 15%) two years later. Budget for the uncertainty.

Duty by origin — China vs. the alternatives

Section 301 is China-only, so where you source changes the effective rate by ~7.5 points before AD even enters the picture.

  • From China: 8% MFN + 7.5% Section 301 = 15.5% combined, plus any A-570-890 deposit on wooden bedroom pieces. Estimate it on the China → US Furniture calculator.
  • From Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia: No Section 301. MFN only — typically 0–12% by piece. These countries picked up share precisely because of the China tariffs, especially in wooden bedroom and office furniture. The infra still trails China's for complex pieces, but on a per-factory basis the math often wins.
  • From Mexico (IMMEX): Nearshoring under the IMMEX program can cut both duty and freight for US-bound furniture. The Mexico Maquila guide explains how the program works.
  • From the UK / EU: The China → UK Homeware page flips the direction; for goods moving into the UK or EU, VAT — not duty — is the dominant line. The Import VAT Guide covers it.

The China math often still works: a solid-wood table at $180 FOB China vs. $240 from Vietnam, plus 15.5% duty on the Chinese one, can still land cheaper. Where it doesn't: bedroom furniture from a factory with a 41%+ AD rate. That's why you run the numbers per factory, per product — not per category.

Compliance that isn't duty — but will stop the container

Furniture draws more agency attention than almost any category. These don't show up in a duty calculator:

  • TSCA Title VI (formaldehyde): If the piece contains composite wood — particleboard, MDF, hardwood plywood — it must meet EPA TSCA Title VI / CARB Phase 2 emission limits, with third-party certifier paperwork. CBP has checked this on Chinese composite-wood furniture with rising frequency since 2022. No certs = a TSCA hold, CF-28 request, and $50–150/day storage while you scramble. Require the docs from the factory before production.
  • CPSC flammability: Mattresses (16 CFR 1633) and upholstered furniture (1640) have burn standards; clothing storage units need tip-over stability compliance (ASTM F2057).
  • Lead in surface coatings: 16 CFR 1303 caps lead in painted/lacquered furniture.
  • USDA APHIS (ISPM 15): Solid wood packing — pallets, crates — must be heat-treated or your whole container can be quarantined.
  • FTC origin & upholstery labeling: Country-of-origin marking and filling-content labels.

The takeaway: furniture compliance isn't one checkbox. It's the right paperwork for every agency with jurisdiction. A broker who knows Chapter 94 will catch the AD exposure and TSCA gap before your container hits the water.

Flat-pack vs. assembled — same duty, different freight

HTS classification doesn't care if furniture ships flat or built; the duty rate is identical. But furniture cubes out before it weighs out, so flat-pack fits 6–8× more units per container. Lower per-unit freight means lower per-unit CIF means lower per-unit duty — on an 8% rate, every $100 cut from freight saves $8 in duty plus the $100 itself. Over a container, that compounds. The trade-off is US-side assembly labor; for DTC brands, flat-pack almost always wins because it offloads assembly to the customer.

The de minimis question — rarely useful here

Section 321 waives duty under $800 goods value, and on paper that sounds useful for furniture. In practice, furniture's bulk kills it. A $600 accent chair plus $200–350 freight technically qualifies on goods value — but once you order a dresser ($400) + nightstand ($200) + bed frame ($350), goods value hits $950 and everything is dutiable. De minimis works for samples and single small pieces; it is not a container-load business model. The jump from informal to formal entry at $800 is also a step-function in cost — bond, broker, MPF — not a gradual ramp. How to actually estimate your landed cost

  1. Pin the 10-digit HTS code — and, for wooden bedroom pieces, the factory's AD rate. This decides MFN, Section 301, and whether A-570-890 applies.
  2. Add freight and insurance for CIF. Furniture's low density means shipping can be 16–30% of factory cost — and it's all dutiable.
  3. Stack the layers: MFN + 7.5% Section 301, plus any AD deposit, plus MPF (0.3464%) and HMF (0.125% on ocean).
  4. Add compliance cost — TSCA certs, CPSC testing, broker fees ($125–250/entry).

Frequently asked questions

What's the real duty rate on imported furniture?

For most pieces: 8% MFN + 7.5% Section 301 = 15.5% combined. Subheadings range 0–12% MFN, with metal seats lowest and rattan highest. Wooden bedroom furniture (9403.50) adds anti-dumping of 0–216% by manufacturer on top of both. The 8% "headline" is a planning number, not what CBP collects.

Why is Chinese wooden bedroom furniture taxed so heavily?

Commerce imposed anti-dumping order A-570-890 in 2005, finding Chinese wooden bedroom furniture sold below fair value. Annual reviews set per-factory cash-deposit rates: cooperating producers got 0%, non-participants got the China-wide rate (up to 216%). It's stayed in place for two decades. Always check the specific factory's rate in ITA ACCESS before ordering — a wrong factory can sink a small business.

Do I need a customs broker for furniture?

For any formal entry (over $2,500), yes — and for furniture, a broker who knows Chapter 94 is worth the $125–250. They'll catch AD exposure before the container sails, flag whether your "cabinet" might be reclassified as bedroom furniture, and handle the TSCA document exchange if you're pulled for a formaldehyde check. A generalist apparel/electronics broker won't know the AD landscape.

Is the $800 de minimis useful for furniture?

Rarely. A single accent chair might qualify on goods value, but furniture's size means you cross $800 of goods value fast, and then the full CIF is dutiable. It works for samples and small pieces, not room-sets. And the 2025–2026 reforms narrowed Section 321 broadly — re-check the 2026 reform page before relying on it.

Does furniture need safety standards beyond duty?

Yes — TSCA Title VI for composite-wood formaldehyde, CPSC flammability (mattresses, upholstery) and tip-over stability, lead limits on coatings, and APHIS heat-treatment for wood packaging. CBP coordinates enforcement with those agencies, and missing paperwork can hold a container for weeks. The specifics depend on the piece, but the pattern is the same: right docs for every agency with jurisdiction.
