UK Import Duty on Chinese Homeware — UKGT at 6.0%

Homeware spans ceramics, glassware, kitchen utensils, tableware, decorative items, and small household goods. Under the UK Global Tariff, the average rate across these categories lands at 6.0%. Some ceramic tableware runs higher at 6-8%, while glassware and kitchen tools often sit at 2-4%. It is a mixed bag, and the exact rate depends entirely on the commodity code you declare — not on what you call the product on your invoice. HMRC's system looks at the 10-digit code, not the product description field.

Work through a real example. Take £600 of ceramic mugs, plates, and kitchen utensils plus £50 shipping and insurance. That gives you £650 CIF. Duty at 6.0% comes to £39.00. Then 20% VAT applies to the duty-paid total of £689.00, producing £137.80 in VAT. Combined government taxes: £176.80. Landed cost: £826.80.

Notice the VAT line is roughly 3.5 times the duty line — importers fixate on the tariff percentage while the real cost driver is the 20% VAT applied on top of an already duty-inflated base. Scale it up: a £2,000 shipment of homeware with £150 freight at the same 6.0% rate generates £129.00 in duty and £455.80 in VAT. That is £584.80 in total border charges before your freight forwarder adds their disbursement fee of £10-25 per customs entry.

Unlike electronics, where duty often sits at 0% under the ITA, homeware attracts a genuine tariff on every pound of goods value above £135. And unlike apparel, where rates can hit 12-16%, the 6.0% average keeps the duty line manageable — but it is not zero, and ignoring it when pricing your goods means taking a margin hit you did not plan for. If you are dropshipping homeware from China to UK consumers, build the duty into your retail price or your customer gets a surprise bill at the door. How HMRC Calculates on Mixed Shipments

Most real homeware shipments are mixed. A typical order might contain ceramic plates (6%), glass tumblers (4%), stainless steel cutlery (3%), and a set of bamboo serving spoons (0%). HMRC requires each distinct commodity code to appear on its own entry line with its own value and its own duty calculation. You cannot average the rates across the whole consignment. A customs broker who lumps everything under one code to save entry-line fees is misdeclaring — and if HMRC audits the entry, the underpaid duty plus interest is your problem, not the broker's.

The £135 De Minimis — Modestly Useful for Small Homeware Orders

The UK applies a £135 threshold for customs duty on low-value consignments — below it, no duty is collected at the border. For small homeware items — a set of bamboo spoons at £12, a ceramic vase at £14, a single glass decanter at £13.50 — a single sample sits comfortably under the line. But most real orders cross £135 quickly, so the threshold mainly helps individual samples and tiny test orders. The cliff edge is sharp: crossing £135 by a single penny triggers full duty on the whole amount, not just the excess above the threshold.

Illustrate it with ceramics. A £134.99 ceramic planter from a Guangdong factory: zero duty. A £135.01 ceramic planter from the same factory, same material, same shipping route: £6.08 in duty at 6.0%. The difference in goods value is two pence. The difference in duty is £6.08. Scale that to a mixed shipment of 200 small kitchen items where the aggregate invoice crosses £135 easily and the threshold becomes irrelevant — but for single-piece samples or small test orders, it genuinely matters.

And the crucial point that trips up first-time importers: VAT at 20% applies either way. There is no VAT de minimis for commercial imports. The £18 low-value VAT exemption was abolished in January 2021 and is not coming back. A £12 bamboo spoon set owes zero duty but still technically owes £2.40 in VAT. In practice, HMRC does not chase individual low-value parcels for VAT, but for any commercial shipment cleared through a broker, the VAT liability is real and collectible. Key UK Commodity Codes for Chinese Homeware

Homeware is not one customs category — it is spread across at least eight different HS chapters. The 10-digit UK commodity code determines your actual duty rate, not the category average we use in the calculator. Getting the code right is the single most important step in your customs entry. Here are the codes that matter most for Chinese homeware imports:

  • 6911 10: Porcelain or china tableware and kitchenware — typically 6%
  • 6912 00: Ceramic tableware, kitchenware, other household articles (non-porcelain) — 4-6%
  • 7013: Glassware for table, kitchen, toilet, office, or indoor decoration — 2-4%
  • 7013 37: Drinking glasses, non-lead crystal — 4%
  • 7323: Iron or steel tableware, kitchenware, and household articles — 2-4%
  • 7323 93: Stainless steel kitchen and household items — 2.7-3.2%
  • 3924: Plastic tableware, kitchenware, and household articles — 6.5%
  • 4419: Wooden tableware and kitchenware — 0-2%
  • 6302: Bed linen, table linen, toilet linen, kitchen linen — ~12% (this is the textile homeware trap — if it is fabric, it costs more)
  • 9405: Lamps, lighting fittings, illuminated signs and nameplates — 2-4%

Check the UK Trade Tariff tool on GOV.UK for the exact rate against the full 10-digit code. A ceramic mug under 6911 10 and a ceramic vase under 6912 00 can land at different rates despite both being "ceramic goods from China." Do not guess — a wrong commodity code means a wrong duty amount, which means an HMRC compliance letter six to eighteen months later requesting payment of the underpaid duty plus interest.

Product Safety for Homeware — What UK Regulators Actually Check

Homeware imports face multiple UK safety regulations, and the applicable rules depend entirely on material and intended use. Kitchenware that contacts food — plates, bowls, mugs, utensils, storage containers — falls under The Materials and Articles in Contact with Food (England) Regulations 2012, with parallel legislation in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. The core requirement: nothing must transfer its constituents to food in quantities that could endanger human health or bring about an unacceptable change in the composition, taste, or odour of the food.

For ceramics specifically, UK regulators test glazes for lead and cadmium migration against limits set in BS EN 1388 and related standards. A ceramic mug with a brightly coloured glaze that has not been properly fired can leach heavy metals into hot liquids — and Trading Standards does pull items from retail shelves for laboratory testing. Plastic kitchenware needs overall migration testing to EN 1186 to prove it does not leach substances above the 10 mg/dm² limit. Melamine kitchenware from unverified Chinese suppliers has been a recurring enforcement problem — some products release formaldehyde above permitted levels when used with hot liquids, and HMRC has detained shipments specifically for melamine testing.

Electrical homeware items — kettles, toasters, coffee machines, table lamps, heated trays — must comply with the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016. That means UKCA marking (CE is still accepted during the extended transition period), compliance with the relevant British Standards, and a technical file you can produce if the regulator asks. The plug itself matters: Chinese factory-standard plugs will not pass a UK port inspection. The plug must be BS 1363 compliant, and fixing non-compliant plugs after arrival costs more than doing it at the source.

Textile homeware — bedding, curtains, cushion covers, upholstered furniture — falls under the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988, which mandate specific flammability performance levels for covers and fillings. A lot of Chinese-manufactured textile homeware sold through online marketplaces does not meet these ignition-resistance standards. Trading Standards periodically pulls product lines for lab testing, and non-compliant goods can be ordered off the market. If your supplier cannot produce test certificates from an ISO 17025 accredited laboratory, get that sorted before the container leaves port. Non-compliant goods at the border can be stopped, detained, and ultimately destroyed — and you eat the loss.

Anti-Dumping on Chinese Ceramics — Check Before You Ship

The UK maintained EU anti-dumping duties on Chinese ceramic tableware and kitchenware post-Brexit under Trade Remedies Authority case TD0007. The rates range from 13.1% to 36.1% depending on the specific manufacturer and exporter. This AD duty sits on top of the standard 6.0% UKGT duty — it is an additional charge, not a substitute. The measure targets tableware and kitchenware specifically: plates, bowls, mugs, cups, saucers, serving dishes, and similar items intended for food use. Decorative ceramic vases, planters, and ornaments are not in scope — the AD order is narrow, but when it applies, the financial impact is large.

Run the math at the top of the AD range. A £600 shipment of ceramic dinner sets: 6.0% duty = £27. 36.1% AD on top = £217. Combined customs charges before VAT: £244 on a £600 goods value — a 40.7% effective tariff rate. VAT at 20% on the £844 duty-paid CIF adds £168.80. Total border charges: £412.80 on a £650 CIF shipment. That is a 63.5% effective tax rate when you express it as a percentage of CIF. At the lower end of the AD range (13.1%), the same shipment pays £78.60 in AD, £105.60 total customs charges before VAT, and roughly £231 total with VAT included — still a 35.5% effective rate.

Some Chinese exporters have individual duty rates below the country-wide residual rate — the TRA publishes a list of cooperating exporters with their specific AD margins. Using an exporter with a lower assigned rate can cut your AD bill by half or more. If your supplier is not on the cooperating list, the residual rate applies. Do that due diligence before the goods ship; you cannot fix it after the container arrives at Felixstowe or London Gateway. See also: China to UK electronics guide.

The IKEA Effect — Why Flat-Pack Furniture Sits Outside "Homeware"

Furniture lives in Chapter 94 and carries its own set of duty rates, typically lower than ceramics and plastic kitchenware. Flat-pack bookshelves, desks, and storage units generally enter at 0-2% under the UKGT, while ceramic plates and plastic storage containers face 4-6.5%. The term "homeware" has no legal meaning in customs — the tariff book cares about material composition and function, not retail category labels.

If your shipment includes both ceramic kitchenware (Chapter 69, ~6%) and a small wooden shelf unit (Chapter 94, ~2%), each item needs its own commodity code on the customs declaration — one entry line per distinct product category. Bundling everything under one commodity code to save paperwork is misclassification. HMRC cross-references commodity codes against commercial invoice line items as a standard audit check. If the system flags a pattern — ceramic code declared but invoice shows wooden furniture — you get a compliance letter.

For repeat importers, HMRC can reassess going back several years. Underpaid duty gets collected with interest. The correct approach: split the declaration, declare the right code per item, and keep the audit trail clean. It takes an extra five minutes per entry and saves you from a much longer conversation with HMRC compliance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the duty rate for homeware from China to the UK?

The UKGT average for homeware sits at 6.0%, but the range is wide and product-specific. Ceramics run 4-6%, glassware 2-4%, plastic kitchenware 6.5%, wooden kitchenware 0-2%, and textile homeware like bedding and table linen hits around 12%. Ceramic tableware may also face anti-dumping duties of 13.1% to 36.1% depending on the exporter, applied on top of the standard UKGT rate. Always check the specific 10-digit UK commodity code for your product — averaging across a mixed shipment works for estimation but not for the actual customs entry.

Do I need special testing for ceramic products from China?

Yes. Food-contact ceramics — plates, bowls, mugs, serving dishes — need lead and cadmium migration testing against the limits in UK food-contact materials regulations. The testing follows BS EN 1388 and related standards, and it must be done by an accredited laboratory. Plastic kitchenware needs overall migration testing to EN 1186 to verify it stays below the 10 mg/dm² limit. Keep the test reports on file — HMRC and Trading Standards can request them during a compliance check, and failing to produce documentation can result in goods being detained at the border or removed from sale. Get certificates from your supplier before shipping; arranging testing after the goods have arrived costs more, takes weeks, and leaves you with inventory you cannot legally sell in the meantime.

Are kitchen knives from China subject to special rules?

Kitchen knives generally fall under Chapter 82 (tools, implements, cutlery) with UKGT rates of 2-4%. The UK has separate import controls on certain bladed articles under the Offensive Weapons Act 2019. Standard kitchen knives — chef's knives, paring knives, bread knives, carving knives — clear normally and do not require special permits. Flick knives, gravity knives, and certain other prohibited weapons cannot be imported at all. If you are bringing in a mixed shipment that includes kitchen knives alongside ceramic plates and glassware, each category needs its own line on the customs entry with the correct commodity code. Do not try to fold the knives into the ceramics declaration to simplify paperwork — the commodity code mismatch will be obvious on audit.

What documentation do I need to clear Chinese homeware through UK customs?

The standard set: a commercial invoice showing seller and buyer details, full goods description, unit and total values, currency, and Incoterms; a packing list with carton-level weights and dimensions; the bill of lading (sea freight) or airway bill (air freight); a valid GB EORI number (free to obtain from HMRC via the government gateway, takes roughly a week to process); and correct 10-digit UK commodity codes for every distinct product line on the invoice. For food-contact items — ceramic plates, plastic utensils, glass drinking vessels — you should also hold a declaration of compliance with UK food-contact materials regulations, supported by the migration test reports discussed above. Missing any one of these documents means your shipment sits in a bonded warehouse accumulating daily storage charges while you chase paperwork across time zones.
