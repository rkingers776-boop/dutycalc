US Import Duty from Thailand, Indonesia & Malaysia

All three countries sit in the same legal corner: no US free trade agreement, no Section 301 exposure, plain MFN rates at the border. The legal position is identical. The commercial reality could hardly be more different — one is a car-parts and hard-drive workhorse, one is a footwear and furniture powerhouse whose biggest export strengths pay the highest tariffs, and one quietly moves more semiconductors into the US than any of them.

Why these three get lumped together — and why that's a mistake

Open any sourcing memo from the past three years and you'll find "ASEAN" treated as a single option, with Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia sharing a paragraph. Legally that's fair: none of the three has a free trade agreement with the US (Singapore is the only ASEAN member that does), and none of them is on the Section 301 list, so everything they ship is assessed at the plain Most Favored Nation rate. The duty consequence of leaving China for any of them is the same — you stop paying the 7.5–25% Section 301 stack and go back to the base tariff. The China tariff guide lays out what that stack looks like.

But the base tariff is where the similarity ends. Thailand's export basket is engineered for low duty: car parts at 2.5%, hard-disk drives and most electronics at zero under the WTO Information Technology Agreement. Indonesia's is engineered for the opposite — the categories it dominates, footwear and apparel, carry some of the highest MFN rates in the schedule. Malaysia sits in between with a semiconductor industry that is effectively tariff-free but has to prove its origin on every entry. If you plan around the region instead of the product, you will misprice all three.

Thailand: the supply-chain play, not the tax play

Nobody moves production to Thailand to save on duty. They move there because the supply chain already works. Thailand builds more than half of the world's hard-disk drives — Seagate and Western Digital both run enormous plants around the eastern seaboard — and it has been the Japanese auto industry's ASEAN backyard for four decades, which is why the country builds close to two million vehicles a year and a dense web of tier-1 suppliers around them.

For a US importer that translates into a handful of genuinely low-duty lanes:

  • Auto parts (Ch. 87) sit at 2.5%, the same MFN Japan and Germany pay — no FTA needed. Finished vehicles (Ch. 8704) are the trap at 25%, so the practical lane is components, not cars.
  • Hard-disk drives and most ITA electronics enter at 0%, though the free line is narrower than people assume — power supplies, cables and non-ITA peripherals run 2.5–5%.
  • Jewelry (Ch. 71) runs 5.5–13.5%, roughly the same profile as India's, and Bangkok's cutting and polishing trade moves a surprising volume of it into the US.
  • Rubber goods (Ch. 40) — tires mostly, and Thailand is the world's largest natural-rubber producer — run 3–6%.
  • Canned tuna and prepared shrimp mostly land at 0–6%.

Two things people get wrong about the Thai corridor. First, the 2.5% auto-parts rate only looks small against China's 301-loaded numbers — against Japan it's the same number, and against Germany it's the same number too, because it's just MFN. Second, Thailand's industrial policy has a habit of changing the ground under you: BOI investment incentives are generous but condition-laden, and the government has periodically imposed local-content requirements on electronics and autos. A Thai supplier's quote is a starting point, not a promise. Get the BOI terms in the contract before you tool up.

Indonesia: the country where the tax map and the factory map point in opposite directions

Indonesia's problem is a nasty coincidence. The product categories where it has real scale — footwear, apparel, furniture — are exactly the categories where US MFN rates are highest, because those are the categories the whole tariff schedule punishes, whoever makes them. The country is the world's second-largest footwear exporter after Vietnam, and most of the big US athletic brands run Indonesian production, yet a mainstream sneaker line still faces around 20% duty, and the footwear chapter (Ch. 64) runs from 6% to 37.5% depending on construction. Apparel is the same story at 14–32%.

That produces a strange arithmetic. A $20,000 sneaker shipment from a Bandung factory, with $1,200 freight and insurance, hits roughly $4,240 in duty before MPF and HMF — an effective load near 22%. The same value in Chinese-made sneakers pays MFN plus Section 301, so Indonesia still wins the comparison against China. But it wins by a smaller margin than the region's cheerleaders suggest, and against Vietnam it can lose outright: Vietnam's shoe lines run the same 20% band, with better ports and faster lead times out of Ho Chi Minh City.

The genuinely cheap Indonesian lanes are the quiet ones: coffee at 0%, rattan and wood furniture mostly 0–8% (see the furniture duty guide), and processed foods at 2–6%. If your product sits in one of those, Indonesia is one of the best-priced corridors in ASEAN. If your product is a sneaker, price the HTS line before you romanticize the wage differential.

There's also a political-economy layer that doesn't show up in the tariff schedule. Indonesia has spent the decade restricting raw-material exports — nickel ore, bauxite, and most recently some copper — to force downstream processing onshore. Those restrictions have created real supply-chain surprises for US buyers of intermediate goods, and Jakarta shows no sign of relaxing them. And because Indonesian customs can be slow and port capacity at Tanjung Priok and Tanjung Perak is chronically tight, budget for dwell time. The duty might be low; the calendar might not be.

Malaysia: the zero-tariff corridor with the paperwork problem

Malaysia is the only one of the three where you can legitimately say the duty is the least interesting part of the deal. The Penang electronics cluster is one of the world's two or three largest semiconductor assembly-and-test centers — the OSAT (outsourced assembly and test) shops in Bayan Lepas handle a meaningful share of the world's chip packaging — and virtually all of that output enters the US at 0% under the ITA, the same zero rate Taiwan gets on chips. Add in rubber gloves (Malaysia makes the majority of the world's, mostly at 0–6%), palm-oil products at 0–6%, and chemicals at 2.5–6.5%, and the country's blended exposure to US tariffs is the lowest in ASEAN. A $50,000 semiconductor shipment to the US pays roughly $243 in fees — MPF and HMF — and zero duty.

The catch is that zero is contingent on proof. ITA duty-free treatment is not automatic: the importer has to substantiate that the product is ITA-covered and that it was substantially transformed in an ITA member country — Malaysia qualifies, which is more than can be said for some of its neighbors. CBP has also been looking hard at the UFLPA angle on electronics, because a chip assembled in Penang from a Xinjiang-origin wafer or polysilicon input carries the same forced-labor exposure as one assembled anywhere. The origin paperwork for Malaysian electronics is not optional decoration; it is the entire game. The UFLPA compliance guide is worth reading before your first entry.

One more lesson from the corridor's own history: the rubber-glove boom and bust of 2020–2021. Glove prices tripled at the pandemic peak, the Malaysian majors (Top Glove, Hartalega, Supermax) sprinted to add capacity, and then demand normalised and margins collapsed. It's a reminder that Malaysian supply chains can swing hard on global demand, and that a 0% duty does not protect you from a bad sourcing decision. The tariff is the easy part; the market is the hard part.

The China+1 comparison, done on one product

Take a concrete case rather than a country average: a $30,000 container of non-excluded industrial pumps, freight and insurance included. From China the tariff stack is 4.2% MFN plus 25% Section 301, or roughly $8,760. From Thailand, the same pumps at the 4.2% MFN that applies to most Chapter 84 machinery: $1,260. That's a $7,500 difference per container, which is the real engine of the whole China+1 story — and it's why Thai machine-tool, Indonesian footwear and Malaysian electronics sourcing all kept growing through 2025 and into 2026 even while overall US import volumes softened.

The comparison cuts the other way on zero-MFN Chinese lines. Where China's own MFN is 0% — certain technical textiles, some industrial machinery, ITA-covered electronics — the ASEAN rate is not automatically cheaper, and the China machinery corridor shows several categories where China wins on paper even after Section 301. The correct habit is to run your actual product through the sourcing comparison tool rather than trusting a regional rule of thumb. For the full US entry process behind whatever number you get, the import customs guide covers the mechanics.

Three traps that show up after the rate quote

Origin integrity. CBP watches for goods routed through ASEAN to dodge Section 301, and it treats Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia the same way it treats Vietnam: goods genuinely manufactured there are fine; re-labeled Chinese goods are fraud, with penalties up to the entered value and the burden of proof on the importer. Keep production records for every PO. If a supplier offers to "ship through" a third country, that is the conversation you end.

De minimis is the same $800 cliff. The Section 321 threshold applies to these three origins exactly as it does to China — under $800 in goods value per person per day, nothing; one dollar over, the whole CIF is dutiable. The mechanics are identical and the rules on splitting orders are identical: structuring to dodge the threshold is fraud. The threshold guide has the full map.

Q3–Q4 capacity. The same container crunch that hits every peak season hits these ports harder, because a smaller share of their traffic runs on US mainline services. Laem Chabang and Port Klang are better connected than Tanjung Priok, but all three tighten up from September. Book space early, and don't treat a confirmed booking as a guarantee of a sailing date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Thailand, Indonesia or Malaysia have a trade agreement with the US?

No. The US has no FTA with any of the three — Singapore is its only ASEAN free-trade partner. Everything from these countries enters under standard MFN rates, and there is no preference certificate to file because there is no preference to claim. If a supplier mentions a "US-Thailand FTA," they are either confused or selling something.

Are these countries hit by Section 301 tariffs?

No. Section 301 under the Trade Act of 1974 is product- and China-specific; goods substantially transformed in Thailand, Indonesia or Malaysia pay only the MFN rate. The exception is origin fraud — if CBP reclassifies goods as Chinese because no real manufacturing happened in the ASEAN country, the full China stack applies retroactively.

Which of the three is cheapest for US-bound imports?

For electronics, Malaysia, by a wide margin — ITA semiconductors at 0% make it the cheapest corridor of the three for chips and components. For most other goods, Thailand's basket (auto parts 2.5%, HDDs 0%) carries lower duty than Indonesia's (footwear up to 37.5%, apparel 14–32%). Indonesia wins on coffee, furniture and food, and on wage levels — not on the tariff schedule.

Why would I choose these over Vietnam?

Vietnam is plain MFN too, so there is no duty advantage among the four — the decision is about capability. Thailand for automotive and HDD supply chains, Malaysia for semiconductor assembly and test, Indonesia for footwear capacity at the lowest wages in the group, Vietnam for apparel density and port speed. Pick by product and factory capability, not by country averages.
