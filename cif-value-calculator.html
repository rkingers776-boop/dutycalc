CIF Value Calculator

Customs does not tax the number on your invoice. It taxes a value it derives from that number, and the derivation depends on which Incoterm the invoice was written under and which country is collecting. A DDP price of $12.50 a unit hides a customs value closer to $10.49; a CIF price of $12.50 contains a goods value lower still. Declaring the headline price instead is the most common valuation error there is, and it always costs money in one direction or the other. This calculator does the conversion in both directions, for all eleven terms.

Two numbers, one invoice

Every import price is a bundle. It contains the goods, the cost of moving them, insurance if someone bought it, and — under DDP — the duty the seller already paid on your behalf. Customs wants exactly one component of that bundle, the price of the goods, and it wants it stated in a way that lets the same rule apply to every importer. So the first job on any entry is to strip the bundle back to its parts, and which parts are inside the price is entirely a function of the Incoterm.

Under EXW, FOB, FCA and FAS the price is essentially the goods alone. Under CFR and CPT it includes the main carriage, so the freight comes out before the customs value is declared. Under CIF and CIP it includes freight and insurance, so both come out. Under DAP and DPU it also includes delivery to the destination, though not import clearance. Under DDP the seller has paid the duty too, which is the one case where the arithmetic is circular — the duty is a percentage of a value that the duty itself is part of — and that is why the DDP case below gets its own treatment.

Which markets assess CIF and which assess FOB

The United States assesses duty on transaction value, which excludes international freight and insurance when they are identified separately on the invoice — 19 U.S.C. 1401a(b)(4)(A). Canada uses the value for duty at the place of direct shipment, which reaches the same place: freight after the shipment point is out. Australia's customs value is an FOB value. In all three, the base is the goods and the freight does not attract duty.

The European Union, the United Kingdom, Japan, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Mexico all assess on a CIF value: goods, plus international freight, plus insurance to the border. Mexico gets there through article 65 of the Ley Aduanera, which adds international freight and insurance into the base rather than adopting a CIF definition outright. The practical consequence is that the same shipment, at the same freight cost, pays more duty entering Rotterdam than it does entering Los Angeles — not because the rate is higher, but because the base is. The customs valuation guide carries the statutory citations for each.

There is a second-order effect that surprises people comparing markets. On high-freight, low-value goods — furniture, bulky plastics, anything where shipping is a third of the cost — the CIF basis can add a fifth to the duty bill. On a laptop where freight is 1% of value it is noise. Which means the comparison between a US and an EU route is not the same comparison for a container of chairs as it is for a pallet of phones, and the landed cost calculator runs both bases side by side for exactly that reason.

The DDP price hides a customs value

When a supplier quotes DDP, the price contains duty on a value you have never seen. Backing it out is straightforward once you accept that duty is not part of the customs value — it is a tax on it — while freight and insurance are also excluded in a goods-value jurisdiction. The price equals the CIF value times one plus the rate, so the CIF value is the price divided by one plus the rate, and the goods value is that less freight and insurance.

Take a $12.50 DDP unit, $1.20 of freight, $0.05 of insurance, and a 6.5% rate. Removing duty first gives $11.74 of CIF value, then $10.49 of goods value. Declare the $12.50 and you have overstated the customs value by $2.01 a unit, which on 20,000 units is $40,200 of value and $2,613 of duty paid that was never owed. The reverse error is more common and worse: a buyer who takes a DDP price at face value when filing their own entry under-declares nothing but mis-prices everything downstream, because their landed cost model now carries a freight figure and a duty figure that do not reconcile.

Insurance is 110% for a reason

Under CIF and CIP the seller must insure the goods, and the 2020 rules are specific about the cover. CIF requires minimum cover at 110% of the contract value under Institute Cargo Clauses C — the narrowest of the standard sets, covering total loss and major casualties but not theft or water damage. CIP requires Clauses A, the widest cover, at the same 110%. The 110% exists because a total loss costs the buyer more than the invoice: they lose the profit on the resale and the cost of the letter of credit as well. The extra 10% is the convention that covers it, and it is also why an insurance figure in a CIF quote is usually a round 0.3–0.5% of value rather than a precise invoice line.

What this means for valuation is narrower than it sounds: the insurance that goes into the CIF value is the premium actually payable for the international leg, not the 110% insured amount. Importers routinely enter the insured sum instead of the premium and inflate their own duty base. The Incoterms guide covers the risk-transfer side of the same eleven terms, which is a different question from who pays what.

Two details in that table cause most of the disputes. Under DAP the seller delivers to a named place but the buyer clears import and pays duty; under DPU the seller also unloads, which is the difference between the two and the reason DPU replaced the old DAT acronym in 2020. And under CIF the risk transfers at the ship's rail even though the seller is paying freight to the destination — insurance and carriage are the seller's cost, not the seller's risk, which is the single most misunderstood thing about CIF.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the customs value the CIF value in the US?

No. The US uses transaction value, which excludes international freight and insurance when they appear separately. If your supplier writes a single delivered price with no breakdown, CBP can treat the freight as part of the price — so the invoice should show it. That is a paperwork habit, not a pricing decision, and it changes the duty base.

Can I just declare the price I paid?

You can, and under most Incoterms you should — the transaction value is the price actually paid or payable. What you cannot do is declare a price that includes things the statute takes out, or omit things it puts in. The price paid is the starting point, not the answer.

What if the goods are shipped to a related company?

Related-party transactions are still valued on the price paid, but the price has to be at arm's length for the goods to be accepted; otherwise customs will use another method. The valuation guide walks the six methods in order.

Does the freight I add for CIF include the destination haulage?

No — only up to the point of importation. Inland carriage after clearance, unloading at the warehouse, and the destination terminal handling charges that often arrive on a separate invoice are all outside the CIF value. Putting them in overstates duty; leaving out the pre-border leg understates it.
