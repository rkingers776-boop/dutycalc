Italian Import Duty on Chinese Textiles — TARIC and the High-Rate Reality

Textiles (Chapters 50-63) carry some of the highest TARIC rates in the EU schedule. The average for Chinese textiles entering Italy works out to 12.0%. That's a composite across yarns, fabrics, knitwear, woven garments, and home textiles — individual codes vary widely across a 5% to 12% range, and for certain sensitive subheadings rates can push even higher. For Italy specifically, as a major textile producer (especially in the Como, Biella, and Prato districts), EU tariff protection on textiles is a sensitive political issue. The Italian textile industry employs over 400,000 people and generates north of €60 billion in annual revenue, so the political will to maintain protective rates on Chinese imports remains strong across governments.

Take a real example: €3,000 of Chinese silk scarves + €200 shipping = €3,200 CIF. Duty at 12.0% = €384. IVA at 22% on €3,584 = €788.48. Total tax: €1,172.48. Landed cost: €4,372.48. That's a 36.6% markup over the original CIF — and it's why accurate duty calculation before you place an order is critical for your margins. We've seen importers who skipped this step and discovered the landed cost had wiped out their entire profit when the goods arrived at Genoa. Don't be that importer — run the numbers first. The calculator above takes seconds and uses the same formula Italian customs agents apply at the border.

What drives the rate variation? Fiber composition is the biggest factor. Cotton and silk tend toward the lower end of the range (5-8%), while synthetic and blended fabrics cluster higher (8-12%). Garment complexity also matters — a simple cotton t-shirt under Chapter 61 might land at 10%, while a lined polyester jacket with multiple fabric types could hit 12%. The lesson: don't guess your HS code. Look it up in TARIC before you ship, or have your Italian customs agent (spedizioniere doganale) confirm it.

Italian IVA at 22% — EU's Highest Standard VAT Rate

Italy applies 22% IVA on imports — one of the highest standard VAT rates in the EU. Only Hungary at 27% and a handful of others are higher. Most other major EU economies sit lower: Germany at 19%, France at 20%, Spain at 21%. IVA is calculated on CIF + duty + any applicable excise (accisa). For IVA-registered Italian businesses, import IVA is recoverable through the monthly IVA return (liquidazione IVA), but the cash-flow hit is real — you pay at customs and recover 30-60 days later through your F24 declaration. For non-EU sellers who aren't IVA-registered in Italy, the 22% cost is permanent and must be priced into your selling price or absorbed by your margin.

One practical tip for regular importers: get IVA-registered in Italy even as a non-EU company. You'll need an Italian fiscal representative (rappresentante fiscale) or register directly if your country has a mutual assistance agreement with Italy. The paperwork takes 4-8 weeks but recovering 22% on every shipment adds up fast. For a business importing €50,000 of textiles per year, that's €11,000 in recoverable IVA annually; for €200,000 per year, it's €44,000. The registration cost (typically €500-1,500) pays for itself within the first shipment or two. One nuance people miss: the IVA base includes the duty — it's 22% of (CIF + duty), not 22% of CIF alone. On a €10,000 CIF shipment at 12.0% duty, that means an extra €264 in IVA. Our calculator handles this correctly using the same formula ADM uses.

The €150 Duty Threshold — and the Abolished €22

Post-July 2021: duty is exempt below €150 goods value (not including shipping). IVA kicks in from €1 via IOSS or customs declaration. For a medium-value textile shipment in the €50-150 range: duty = 0, but IVA at 22% still applies on the full CIF value. For the typical textile importer bringing in bolts of fabric or garment lots, the €150 duty threshold is quickly exceeded — a single roll of decent cotton shirting fabric runs €200-400, so duty almost always applies on commercial textile shipments.

The €22 figure is the old de minimis threshold for both duty and VAT that was abolished in July 2021. You'll still see it referenced in older guides and on some Chinese supplier websites, but it's gone. Today, €22 is only tangentially relevant — it's the threshold below which some member states waive the customs declaration fee, but the substantive duty and VAT exemptions at €22 no longer exist. IOSS for B2C Textile Sales into Italy

Same EU-wide IOSS rules apply here — textiles don't get any special treatment under IOSS. For B2C shipments under €150: register in one EU state (Ireland and the Netherlands are the most common choices for non-EU sellers because of their English-language support and established intermediary sectors), charge Italian IVA at 22% at checkout, and remit monthly through the IOSS portal. For shipments over €150: standard import procedure with IVA paid at the border via the customs agent. Italy's Agenzia delle Dogane e dei Monopoli (ADM) handles customs clearance nationwide through its network of dogane principali and dogane secondarie.

If you're doing B2C textile sales into Italy at any volume, IOSS is worth it — it means your Italian customers don't get hit with surprise customs fees and IVA demands on delivery, which dramatically reduces returns and chargebacks. Italian consumers are particularly sensitive to unexpected customs charges; DHL and Poste Italiane will hold the package and send a payment demand letter (bollettino) before releasing it, which can add 1-2 weeks to delivery time. Check our guides for France and Germany — the IOSS mechanics are identical across the EU but the VAT rates and local customs cultures differ meaningfully.

Italian Customs Clearance and Key Ports

Major entry points for Chinese textiles: Gioia Tauro (the Mediterranean transshipment hub — it's the busiest container port in Italy by TEU volume, handling roughly 3.5 million TEUs annually, and acts as a relay point where containers from mega-vessels are split onto feeders for smaller Italian and Mediterranean ports), Genoa (northern Italy's main gateway, serving the Milan textile and fashion district with direct rail links to the Lombardy industrial zone — the Voltri-Pra terminal handles the bulk of Chinese container traffic), Naples (strong for Campania-region garment manufacturing, especially in the Nola and Caserta industrial areas), and Venice (serving the Veneto footwear and textile cluster around Treviso and Montebelluna). Air freight comes through Milan Malpensa (MXP) for high-value fashion samples, prototype runs, and express shipments that can't wait for sea freight's 28-35 day transit from major Chinese ports like Shanghai, Ningbo, or Shenzhen.

Clearance runs through the AIDA (Automazione Integrata Dogane Accise) electronic system. Italian customs is thorough on textile entries — they check fiber composition against declared HS codes, country of origin marking, and valuation carefully. Expect to provide commercial invoices with HS codes at the 8-10 digit TARIC level, detailed packing lists with weights and fiber contents, and certificates of origin for every shipment. Random physical inspections (visita merce) are common for textile entries from China — roughly 5-10% of shipments get pulled for examination, and if your labels or documentation don't match the goods, clearance can stall for 1-3 weeks while you sort it out. Italian customs brokerage fees run €80-200 per formal entry depending on complexity, the port, and the spedizioniere you use. Genoa and Gioia Tauro tend toward the lower end because of volume; smaller ports and airports toward the higher end.

Key TARIC Codes for Chinese Textiles into Italy

  • Chapters 50-55: Yarns and fabrics (silk, wool, cotton, synthetic) — 4-8% typical. Silk (Chapter 50) tends toward 4-6%; synthetic filament yarns (Chapter 54) cluster around 6-8%; cotton fabrics (Chapter 52) range from 6-12.0% depending on weave and weight.
  • Chapter 60: Knitted fabrics — 6-8%. This is a huge category for Italian sportswear and casual-wear manufacturers sourcing from China. Includes both warp-knit and weft-knit fabrics.
  • Chapter 61: Knitted garments — 10-12% (Italy's highest-volume textile import category from China by value). T-shirts, sweaters, activewear, knit dresses, and hosiery all fall here. The rate variation depends on fiber composition and garment type.
  • Chapter 62: Woven garments — 9.5-12%. Shirts, trousers, jackets, suits, and coats. The rate varies by fiber composition and garment type. Cotton shirts tend toward 9.5-10%; synthetic and blended outerwear toward 12%.
  • Chapter 63: Made-up textile articles (bedding, curtains, household linen) — 6.5-12%. Lower rates for basic cotton bedding; higher for technical or multi-layer items like blackout curtains or padded mattress covers.
  • Italy-specific: Prato district importers use 5112 (woven wool fabrics) and 6006 (knitted fabrics) heavily — these two codes account for a significant share of Chinese textile imports into Tuscany. Como's silk district runs heavily on Chapter 50 codes.

Made in Italy and Country of Origin Rules

Italy has strict enforcement on "Made in Italy" labeling — stricter than almost any other EU member state. Chinese textiles that undergo substantial transformation in Italy — for example, Chinese fabric that's cut and sewn in Italy — can qualify for "Made in Italy" under EU non-preferential origin rules, because the cut-and-sew operation changes the HS chapter (this is the "last substantial transformation" test). This is the standard the EU applies, and it's why many Italian fashion brands can legitimately use "Made in Italy" even when fabric sourcing is global. The key legal concept is that the operation must be economically justified and result in a product with properties and composition different from the input materials.

But merely finishing, dyeing, pressing, or adding buttons and zippers to Chinese-made garments does not change origin — these are considered minor operations (operazioni minime) under EU customs law and do not confer origin. ADM and the Guardia di Finanza actively investigate fraudulent Made in Italy claims. Penalties under Italian law (Legge 166/2009 and the Penal Code provisions on commercial fraud — frode in commercio, Articles 515-517 of the Codice Penale) can be severe: fines up to €50,000, confiscation of the entire shipment, and in egregious cases, criminal charges carrying prison sentences. If your supply chain involves Chinese inputs and Italian finishing, work with an Italian trade lawyer or a doganalista to determine the correct origin label before you ship. The cost of getting it wrong — both financially and reputationally — is far higher than the cost of getting the legal advice upfront. This isn't the place to cut corners.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the duty rate for Chinese textiles entering Italy?

The average TARIC rate is 12.0%. Knitted and woven garments (Chapters 61-62) run 10-12% — the highest band. Yarns and fabrics (Chapters 50-55) sit at 4-8%. Home textiles under Chapter 63 range from 6.5% to 12% depending on the specific article. The rate varies significantly by HS chapter and specific TARIC subheading — always check the exact 10-digit TARIC code before shipping, ideally with an Italian customs agent who knows the schedule and can flag any anti-dumping duties or quotas that may also apply to your product.

Can Chinese textiles qualify as "Made in Italy"?

Yes — if they undergo substantial transformation in Italy. Cut-and-sew operations that change the HS chapter (e.g., fabric from Chapter 52 or 54 into a garment under Chapter 61 or 62) qualify as origin-conferring under EU rules. Dyeing, finishing, pressing, steam-setting, or minor assembly of Chinese-made garments does not qualify — these are operazioni minime. The Guardia di Finanza actively investigates fraudulent claims, and penalties can reach €50,000 plus confiscation of goods. Many legitimate Italian fashion houses use globally sourced fabrics (including Chinese) with Italian construction and correctly label "Made in Italy" — the key is the transformation, not the fiber source. But document everything: keep records of cutting slips, payroll records for the workers who did the assembly, and factory floor documentation that proves the work happened in Italy.

What's the IVA rate on Chinese textile imports to Italy?

22% standard Italian IVA, calculated on CIF + duty. It's fully recoverable for IVA-registered businesses through the monthly liquidazione IVA (F24 form), but there's a 30-60 day gap between customs payment and recovery. Non-EU companies can register for Italian IVA through a fiscal representative (rappresentante fiscale) and recover it the same way Italian companies do. For non-registered importers, 22% is a permanent cost you need to price into your landed cost calculation from the start. One thing to watch: if you're importing into Italy but your business is in another EU country, you may be able to use your home country's VAT number and account for the import under the reverse charge mechanism (inversione contabile) instead of paying IVA at the border. Talk to your accountant about whether this applies to your situation.

Do I need special permits to import textiles from China to Italy?

No special import permits or licenses are required for standard textiles. But you do need: EU REACH chemical compliance (especially for synthetic textiles with chemical treatments, dyes, or finishes — azo dyes are restricted and tested at the border by ADM labs), fiber composition labels in Italian (etichettatura di composizione tessile as required by EU Regulation 1007/2011 — labels must list all fibers in descending order of weight percentage, using standardized Italian fiber names), and care labeling symbols per ISO 3758. If you're importing leather goods or textile products with leather components, additional CITES restrictions and chemical requirements under REACH Annex XVII may apply. If your textiles contain or are treated with biocides (anti-mold, anti-bacterial treatments), you may need Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR) compliance documentation. The ADM can and will hold shipments at the border for labeling non-compliance — it's one of the most common reasons for clearance delays on textile entries from China.
