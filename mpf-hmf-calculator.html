MPF and HMF Calculator

Two charges on a US entry are not duty, are not optional, and almost never appear in the rate a broker quotes you over the phone. The merchandise processing fee is 0.3464% of the entered value held between a floor and a ceiling that CBP resets every October. The harbor maintenance fee is 0.125% of the same value, charged only when the cargo arrives by sea. Together they run anywhere from 0.47% to well over 1% of the shipment's value depending on its size — which is exactly why they deserve a calculator instead of a line item called "misc".

The floor and the cap decide more than the rate does

The ad valorem rate has not moved in years: 0.3464% of entered value, set by 19 CFR 24.23 and confirmed unchanged in the FY2027 fee notice. What moves every October is the pair of limits around it, and those limits are what make the fee behave strangely at both ends of the size range. At the small end, 0.3464% does not reach the $34.58 minimum until the shipment is worth about $9,983 — so a $5,000 entry pays 0.69% of its value, and a $500 spare-parts shipment pays nearly 7%. The floor is the same money whether the box holds a thousand dollars of goods or ten thousand, which is why consolidating small shipments is often worth more than a better freight rate.

At the large end the fee stops growing. The $670.86 maximum binds from roughly $193,666 of entered value upward, so a $400,000 container pays 0.17% and a $2 million one pays 0.03%. Between those two numbers the fee is a genuine percentage and the arithmetic is uninteresting — which is most of the range most importers live in, and probably why the floor and the ceiling get so little attention until they matter.

Formal, informal, and the line at $2,500

An informal entry pays a flat fee instead of the percentage: $2.77 when it is filed electronically and not prepared by CBP, $8.30 when it is filed on paper, $12.45 when a CBP officer prepares it. Informal entry is available up to $2,500 in value under 19 CFR 143.21, and the arithmetic looks obviously favourable — $2.77 against a $34.58 floor is a saving of $31.81 on the entry. Filing formal anyway sometimes still makes sense, because formal entry is what gives you a bond, a preference claim, and a liquidation record you can protest, but the gap should be a decision rather than a surprise.

There is one carve-out worth knowing if you are importing from China. Articles valued over $250 that are classified in Chapter 99 subchapters III and IV — which is where the Section 301 China lines and the forced-labour line sit — cannot use informal entry even when the shipment as a whole is under $2,500. A single $300 item on a 9903.88 line pushes the whole entry to formal, and the $34.58 floor comes with it. The duty stack calculator is the right place to work out what that entry owes in duty before you decide how to file it.

HMF is the ocean-only one

The harbor maintenance fee is 0.125% of the value of commercial cargo unloaded from a vessel at a covered US port, under 19 CFR 24.24. It has no minimum and no maximum, which makes it the simplest of the two fees to predict and, at scale, the larger one: on a $400,000 container it is $500, more than three times the capped MPF. Air, truck and rail shipments pay nothing here, which is one of the quieter reasons air freight is less disadvantaged than its headline rate suggests on high-value, low-weight goods.

Two wrinkles come up often enough to mention. Cargo admitted into a foreign trade zone pays HMF at admission rather than at entry, reported quarterly on CBP Form 349 — so an FTZ does not remove the fee, it moves the moment. And unlike MPF, no trade agreement waives HMF; a USMCA-qualifying shipment that pays zero MPF still pays 0.125% if it arrives by sea.

Who pays no MPF at all

19 CFR 24.23(c) lists the programs whose originating goods are exempt from both tiers. USMCA, the US-Israel FTA, CBERA, GSP least-developed beneficiaries, and products of US insular possessions are exempt outright. A second group — Singapore, Chile, Australia, Bahrain, CAFTA-DR, Oman, Peru, Korea, Colombia, Panama — is exempt from the formal fee and the manual surcharge but not from the informal flat fee. Agreements that are not on the list, Jordan and Morocco among them, remove nothing. When an entry mixes qualifying and non-qualifying goods, the fee is computed on the non-qualifying value only, still subject to the floor.

Which means the interesting question on a USMCA shipment is not "is duty zero" — usually it is — but whether the origin claim is documented well enough to also zero the $34.58. On a thousand small entries a year that is $34,580 of avoidable cost, and it is the kind of number that only shows up when someone actually runs the arithmetic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are these fees deductible from the duty I owe?

No. Duty, MPF and HMF are separate charges paid together at entry, and the fees are not computed on the duty or the other way round — both use the entered value. AD/CVD is separate again.

Do the FY2027 figures apply to my entry?

They apply to entries dated 1 October 2026 through 30 September 2027. An entry filed on 30 September 2026 gets the FY2026 limits of $33.58 and $651.50, and the difference on a large shipment is $19.36 — small, but it appears on every reconciliation if the cut-off was handled carelessly.

Why is my broker's MPF different from this number?

Three usual causes: the broker is computing on a different value than you are (they use the entered value, which can include assists and packing you left off), a manual filing added the $4.15, or the entry includes goods that qualify for an agreement you did not claim. The fee itself is not negotiable; the base and the exemption are.

Does de minimis affect these fees?

The old $800 exemption is gone for all countries as of 29 August 2025, so there is no longer a value band where a commercial shipment escapes both duty and fees. What remains is the informal-entry route for shipments of $2,500 or less, which changes the fee amount but not whether one is owed. The threshold guide keeps the current position per market.
