Country of Origin: The Question That Decides Every Duty Rate

Two importers bring in the same product. Same HTS code, same entered value, same port, same week. One pays $20,000 in duty. The other pays $5,000. Nothing about the goods is different, and neither importer made a classification error. The only variable is one line on the entry: country of origin.

Origin used to be a packaging question. It is now the most consequential single field you file, because it is what selects the layers sitting on top of your HTS rate — the Section 301 China lists, the forced-labour Section 301 action that took effect on 24 July 2026, antidumping and countervailing duty orders, and the Section 232 metals measures. Get classification right and origin wrong and you still pay the wrong number, usually for years before anyone notices.

Here is how the determination actually works: the substantial transformation test, the two places where tariff shift rules replace it, why the same shipment can legitimately carry two different countries of origin at once, and what a wrong claim costs once CBP comes back to it.

Short version
  • For most goods, origin is decided by substantial transformation — did the processing produce an article with a new name, character or use.
  • Two carve-outs run on tariff shift rules instead: goods of Canada and Mexico (19 CFR 102.11), and textiles and apparel from anywhere (19 CFR 102.21).
  • One shipment can have two origins. Marking origin and trade-remedy origin are different determinations, and both can be correct.
  • Preferential origin is a separate exam. Qualifying for USMCA does not settle whether Section 301 applies.
  • A wrong claim costs the duty, a 10% marking duty that cannot be remitted, penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1592, and 40% on goods found transshipped to evade duty.

One word, three different questions

“Country of origin” sounds like a single fact. It is not. It is a label applied to three separate legal determinations, each with its own test, and the answer to one does not answer the others. Almost every expensive origin mistake comes from assuming they are the same thing.

Question Test applied What it controls
Non-preferential origin Substantial transformation, except where the Part 102 tariff shift rules apply Section 301 lists, the 2026 forced-labour tiers, AD/CVD orders, quota, and origin marking
Preferential origin The product-specific rule of origin in the agreement: a tariff shift, a regional value content threshold, or a specified process Whether you get the FTA rate instead of the MFN rate
Section 232 metals origin Where the metal was melted and poured, plus how much metal the article contains Whether the metals measure reaches your article at all, and at what rate

The layering is what makes this worth getting right. Your HTS code sets the base rate. Origin then decides which of the additional layers switch on, and at what level — and since 24 July 2026 those layers are no longer mostly a China question. The forced-labour Section 301 action covers 60 economies at four different levels, so misstating origin between two non-China countries now changes the bill where it once changed nothing.

A $50,000 shipment, four origins, four answers. Take a line carrying a 2.5% MFN rate and an entered value of $50,000, and hold everything constant except origin. As a product of China it carries MFN plus the 25% Section 301 list rate plus the additive 12.5% forced-labour tier: $20,000. As a product of Vietnam, no list rate but the same additive 12.5% tier: $7,500. As a product of Mexico, MFN plus the additive 10% tier: $6,250. As a product of an EU member state, filled up to the 10% floor: $5,000. Duty only, before MPF and fees. The gap between the first and second line is 25 percentage points, and it is created entirely by where the goods were made.

Substantial transformation: what the test actually asks

The default rule is in the regulations, not in a statute. 19 CFR 134.1(b) says that further work or material added to an article in another country must effect a substantial transformation to make that other country the country of origin. The test itself comes from the courts: an article emerges from a process with a new name, character or use, different from what it had going in.

That formulation is over a century old, and it is deliberately open-ended. CBP decides each case on the totality of the evidence, which is why two factories doing apparently similar work can get opposite answers. What the decided cases add is a set of questions CBP will ask regardless of the product:

  • How much work was done? The extent of the operations is the determinative issue — the number of components, the number of distinct operations, and whether real time, skill and quality control were involved. National Hand Tool Corp. v. United States, 16 CIT 308 (1992).
  • Did the inputs lose their identity? If the parts are still recognisably themselves inside the finished article, they probably were not transformed. Belcrest Linens v. United States, 573 F. Supp. 1149 (1983).
  • Was the change more than cosmetic? Where a process is a minor one that leaves the identity of the article intact, there is no transformation. Uniroyal, Inc. v. United States, 3 CIT 220 (1982).
  • What carries the essence of the finished good? Courts have looked at which component imparts the essential character, and at whether assembly was complex or merely simple. United States v. Gibson-Thomsen Co., 27 C.C.P.A. 267 (1940).

Read together, those questions are stricter than the three-word slogan suggests. “New name, character or use” is the headline; “the extent of the operations” is what actually decides the case. A nine-step process can still fail if every step leaves the fundamental character of the input untouched.

Nine processes, and the origin did not move. In 2020 CBP ruled that bovine hides tanned to wet-blue state in Brazil and then finished in Vietnam — splitting, shaving, neutralising, dyeing, fatliquoring, conditioning, staking, buffing and finishing — emerged as a product of Vietnam. Six years later CBP revoked itself. In HQ H355209, published in the Customs Bulletin in August 2026, the same operations were held to be “cosmetic in nature and akin to finishing operations,” and the origin became Brazil. The revocation applies to goods entered for consumption on or after 18 October 2026. Nothing about the factory changed. CBP changed its mind about what that factory was doing.

What generally does not confer origin

The pattern across rulings is consistent enough to plan around. These are the operations that most often get claimed as transformation and most often fail:

Operation Typical result Why
Repackaging, relabelling, kitting No change of origin Nothing about the article changed — only what it arrived in
Simple assembly: screwing, bolting, gluing pre-finished parts Usually no change Components keep their identity; the assembly adds no new character
Cutting to length, bending, folding Usually no change Dimension changes are not character changes
Dyeing, painting, coating, finishing Usually no change Treated as cosmetic where the substrate is already the finished article
Mixing or blending No change unless chemically distinct A blend that is still recognisably its inputs has not been transformed
Transit through a hub, with documentation only No change The shipping route is not the origin

And on the other side, the operations that usually do confer origin: weaving yarn into fabric, converting steel coil into a machined part, populating a bare printed circuit board, and chemical reactions that produce a genuinely different substance. The dividing line is not the number of steps and not the factory’s address. It is whether the thing that comes out is a different article of commerce from the thing that went in.

Where tariff shift replaces the test

Two categories never reach the substantial transformation analysis at all. For both, CBP applies codified tariff shift rules under 19 CFR Part 102 instead — you look up your HTS classification in a table, and the table tells you what has to happen for origin to change.

Category Rule set How origin is decided
Goods of Canada and Mexico 19 CFR 102.11 A hierarchy: wholly obtained, then the specified tariff shift for the heading, then regional value content
Textiles and apparel, any origin 19 CFR 102.21 Wholly assembled, then a tariff shift, then a hierarchy of tie-breakers
Everything else Substantial transformation Case by case, on the totality of the evidence

The practical difference is predictability. Under a tariff shift rule you can determine origin before you ship, because the answer is written down. Under substantial transformation you are predicting how CBP will weigh your facts, which is why the ruling request exists — and why so many supply chains that looked defensible on paper turned out not to be.

The same entry, two countries, both correct. Because the Part 102 rules govern marking for goods of Canada and Mexico while substantial transformation governs trade remedies, a single shipment can be marked Made in Mexico and assessed Section 301 duty as a product of China. Importers discover this when the marking is accepted and the duty bill is not. It is not an inconsistency in the law so much as two different laws, and CBP has floated unifying them several times since 1991 without ever finalising it.

Preferential origin is a separate exam

Everything above decides non-preferential origin. If you want a preferential rate under USMCA or another agreement, you have to pass a second and unrelated test: the product-specific rule of origin for your tariff line. That rule is usually one of three things.

  • A tariff shift — every non-originating material must change to the required heading, subheading or tariff item.
  • A regional value content threshold — under USMCA, generally 60% by transaction value or 50% by net cost, calculated on the agreed formula.
  • A specified process — a particular manufacturing step that must happen in the territory.

There is a de minimis allowance, generally letting non-originating materials that fail the tariff shift through at 10% of transaction value, with a different basis for textiles. And there is a critical limit on what passing buys you: a preference claim answers whether you get the FTA rate. It does not answer whether Section 301 applies, whether the forced-labour tiers apply, or whether an AD/CVD order covers the goods. Each of those measures has its own origin rule, and most of them point back to substantial transformation.

The EU and UK run the same split. Non-preferential origin under Article 60 of the Union Customs Code asks where the last substantial, economically justified processing took place, in an undertaking equipped for it, resulting in a new product or an important stage of manufacture. Preferential origin under a trade agreement is a separate determination again, with its own product-specific rules, its own statement on origin and its own registered-exporter mechanics. If you ship both ways, read the EU–UK TCA origin rules alongside this page rather than assuming the tests map onto each other.

Marking: the 10% duty nobody budgets for

Marking is where origin stops being a calculation and becomes a physical object on your goods. 19 U.S.C. 1304 requires every article of foreign origin to be marked with the English name of its country of origin, in a conspicuous place, legibly, indelibly and permanently, in a way that tells the ultimate purchaser in the United States where the article came from.

Four of those words do the work. Conspicuous means the buyer sees it without hunting. Indelible means it survives handling. Permanent means it survives the product. And English is not satisfied by a two-letter country code, or by “Made in PRC”.

Get it wrong and 19 U.S.C. 1304(i) levies a duty of 10% ad valorem. The statute then closes every exit: the duty is deemed to have accrued at importation, is not penal in character, and shall not be remitted wholly or in part, nor shall payment be avoided for any cause. On the $50,000 shipment above, that is $5,000 with no relief mechanism. The one route out is to mark, export, or destroy the goods under CBP supervision before the entry liquidates — all three cost money, and all three require you to act in time.

Defacing a mark is a crime, not a civil penalty. Under 19 U.S.C. 1304(l), removing or defacing a country of origin mark carries a fine of up to $100,000 or a year in prison for a first offence, rising to $250,000 for a subsequent one. That is aimed at people who take the mark off, not at paperwork errors — but it is the reason “we will just re-stick the label at the warehouse” is not a compliance plan.

Transshipment: 40%, and mitigation is barred by the order itself

Routing goods through a third country is not illegal. Plenty of legitimate supply chains consolidate in a regional hub. What is penalised is transshipment undertaken to disguise origin — and here the penalty is written into an executive order rather than left to the tariff schedule.

Section 3 of Executive Order 14326 (31 July 2025) provides that an article CBP determines was transshipped to evade applicable duties is subject to an additional 40% ad valorem rate, in lieu of the rate that would otherwise apply, plus any other applicable fine or penalty including those under 19 U.S.C. 1592, plus every other duty owed on goods of the true country of origin. Then the sentence that matters most: CBP shall not allow mitigation or remission of those penalties. The order also directs Commerce, DHS and USTR to publish, every six months, a list of countries and specific facilities used in circumvention schemes.

Because the trigger is evasion rather than routing, intent is inferred from objective circumstances. The questions are the ones you would ask if you were suspicious: could the intermediate facility actually do what was claimed, does its capacity match the export volumes, do production records exist for the period, and do the utility bills and payroll data support a factory running at that scale. Encryption of the paper trail is the usual failure mode — a clean certificate of origin sitting on top of nothing.

Enforcement capacity behind that rule has grown. Executive Order 14411 (3 June 2026), “Strengthening Customs Enforcement”, names rules of origin and origin marking among the laws it directs CBP to enforce, raises importer eligibility and bonding requirements, and pulls brokers into the due diligence obligation. An administration report published in August 2026 estimated that at least $34.2 billion of goods are transshipped into the United States annually; treat that as an estimate rather than a measurement, but note what it signals about where the audit effort is going. CBP’s published position through October 2026 is reflected here.

Build the file before you need it

Origin is a factual claim you make on entry and defend years later. The recordkeeping rules in 19 CFR Part 163 require five years, and an origin determination is only as good as the evidence you can still produce on year four. Assemble it once, per part number:

  • Bill of materials with an origin on every line, including sub-components the tier-one supplier bought in.
  • Process flow with cycle times, showing what happens where, and how long it takes.
  • Capacity evidence: machine list, headcount, floor area — enough to show the claimed work could have been done there.
  • Production records for the period: work orders, output logs, inspection records.
  • Corroboration that is hard to fake: utility consumption, payroll data, raw material receipts matched to output.
  • Photographs or video of the actual operations, dated.

A supplier certificate saying “Made in Vietnam” is not evidence of substantial transformation. It is a statement that someone else believes it. When the question is whether nine finishing steps changed the character of an input, the answer lives in the production records, not in the certificate.

How to sanity-check your own answer

Before you file, run the determination through four questions in order. They will not give you certainty, but they will catch most of the claims that later collapse:

  • Does a codified rule govern this good? Canada, Mexico, textiles and apparel: look up the Part 102 rule first. The answer is written down; use it.
  • If not, what is the article before and after? Name one. If your honest answer is “the same thing, finished”, you do not have a transformation.
  • Which layer am I trying to switch off? Find the rule for that measure specifically. A USMCA qualification is not a Section 301 answer.
  • Can I prove it in year four? If the evidence does not exist yet, create it now or price the risk.

Then price the outcome both ways. Run the same shipment through the import duty calculator under each candidate origin, and check the full picture including freight and fees with the landed cost calculator. If the spread between two origins is large enough to change your sourcing decision, it is large enough that CBP will look at the claim — and large enough that you should want the file to be airtight rather than merely plausible.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is substantial transformation?

Substantial transformation is the US test for non-preferential country of origin. Processing in a country confers origin only if the article that emerges has a new name, character or use, different from what went in. CBP decides it on the totality of the evidence, case by case. Simple assembly, repackaging, relabelling and finishing operations usually fail the test, which is why goods routed through a third country normally keep the origin of the country where the real manufacturing happened.

Is country of origin the country the goods shipped from?

No. Origin is where the goods were made, not the last port of loading and not where the seller is based. A container shipped from a distribution hub in Singapore carries the origin of wherever the goods were last substantially transformed. Shipping route and origin are separate facts, and CBP treats a mismatch between them as a reason to look closer.

Can one product have two different countries of origin at the same time?

Yes, and this is normal rather than an error. For goods of Canada and Mexico, the origin used for marking is determined under the tariff shift hierarchy in 19 CFR 102.11, while the origin used for Section 301 and other trade remedies is determined under the substantial transformation test. The same entry can therefore be marked Made in Mexico and assessed Section 301 duty as a product of China.

Does a USMCA preference claim change my country of origin for Section 301?

No. Preferential origin and non-preferential origin are separate determinations. Passing the USMCA product-specific rule of origin earns the USMCA rate of duty; it does not by itself decide whether a Section 301, forced-labour Section 301 or AD/CVD measure applies. Each measure has its own origin rule, and several of them point back to substantial transformation.

What does a wrong country of origin cost?

Several things stack. The duty shortfall itself, with interest. A marking duty of 10% ad valorem under 19 U.S.C. 1304(i) if the goods were not properly marked, which the statute says cannot be remitted. Penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1592 for a false statement, which reach up to the value of the goods for a culpable violation. And for goods found to have been transshipped to evade duty, Executive Order 14326 imposes an additional 40% ad valorem rate and bars CBP from mitigating or remitting the penalties.

How long do I need to keep origin records?

Five years from the date of entry, under the recordkeeping rules in 19 CFR Part 163. That clock is the reason to build the origin file at the time you make the claim rather than after CBP asks: the production records, utility bills and payroll data that prove a factory actually did the work are much harder to assemble years later, and a supplier affidavit on its own is not evidence of substantial transformation.
