The Netherlands as the EU's China Import Gateway

Rotterdam is Europe's largest container port, handling roughly 30% of all China-to-EU container traffic — over 14 million TEUs annually. A massive share of Chinese goods destined for the EU market enter through the Netherlands, even goods ultimately sold in Germany, France, or Poland. Because the EU is a customs union, goods clear Dutch customs once and move freely to any other member state without further customs formalities. This single-clearance advantage is the core of the Rotterdam hub model: you clear once in the Netherlands and distribute anywhere in the EU's 450-million-consumer market.

The Dutch customs authority, Douane Nederland, is business-friendly and efficient, processing millions of declarations annually through the automated AGS system. The average TARIC rate is 3.5% for general cargo from China. Dutch BTW (VAT) is 21%, the standard rate. Here's a real-world example: €5,000 goods + €250 shipping = €5,250 CIF. Duty at 3.5% = €183.75. BTW at 21% on €5,433.75 = €1,141.09. Total charges: €1,324.84. Landed cost: €6,574.84. Dutch logistics providers offer integrated warehousing, pick-and-pack, and last-mile delivery across the EU, making the Netherlands the default entry point for Chinese exporters serving the European market at scale.

Article 23 — Customs Warehouse Deferral

The Netherlands' biggest customs advantage: Article 23 (douaneregeling 42) allows importers to defer both duty and VAT at the border and pay VAT through their periodic Dutch VAT return rather than at customs. This is a massive cash-flow advantage and one of the primary reasons so many non-EU companies choose Dutch fulfillment centers over alternatives in Germany, France, or Belgium. Goods enter a customs warehouse, clear customs duty, but the VAT line moves from the border to your VAT return. You declare the import BTW as both input and output on the same return — net zero cash flow impact for VAT-registered businesses.

Without Article 23, you'd need to front 21% of CIF-plus-duty at the border and wait weeks or months for the refund. With it, the cash simply never leaves your account. Here's the real-world math: on a €100,000 CIF shipment with 3.5% duty, the duty is €3,500 and the BTW on the €103,500 duty-paid value is €21,735. Without Article 23, you pay €25,235 at the border and recover the €21,735 BTW through your next VAT return — a cash-flow float of nearly €22,000. With Article 23, you pay only the €3,500 duty at the border and the €21,735 VAT moves to your return where it nets to zero. Scale that across 20 containers a year and the working-capital implications are serious.

The Article 23 license (vergunning artikel 23) requires a separate application through the Belastingdienst — it is not automatic with your BTW registration. Your customs broker can coordinate the paperwork, which typically takes 2–4 weeks after your BTW number is active. The main requirements: you must have an active Dutch BTW registration, you must maintain a customs administration (douaneadministratie) that tracks goods through the warehouse, and you must file periodic VAT returns on time. If you fall behind on VAT filings, the Belastingdienst can and will suspend the Article 23 license — and all your import VAT becomes immediately payable at the border.

Rotterdam alone has over 200 bonded warehouses (douane-entrepots) operated by logistics providers who specialize in Article 23 operations. Two main types matter for Chinese importers: Type D (private bonded warehouse where the warehouse keeper acts as the declarant and guarantees the duties) and Type E (simplified procedure where the goods remain in your own premises under customs supervision). For most e-commerce and fulfillment operations, Type D through a 3PL is the practical path — it's turnkey, the 3PL handles all customs interactions, and you focus on selling. Type E is typically reserved for large importers with their own warehousing infrastructure and dedicated customs compliance staff.

One thing that burns first-time users: Article 23 defers the BTW, not the duty. You still pay the TARIC duty at the border on every entry. Only the VAT component moves to the periodic return. Some importers confuse this and assume the entire border charge is deferred — it's not. Duty is always due at clearance. Also, Article 23 only applies to goods that remain in the customs warehouse or are re-exported. If goods are released to free circulation in the Netherlands (i.e., sold to a Dutch customer), the VAT becomes due on that transaction through the standard VAT return cycle. The deferral is temporary — it shifts the VAT obligation from the border to your accounting period, but it doesn't eliminate it unless the goods are re-exported outside the EU. The €22/€150 Split and Dutch IOSS

Same EU-wide rules apply in the Netherlands: duty exempt below €22, VAT from €1 via IOSS or border collection since the July 2021 abolition of the low-value VAT exemption. For Dutch fulfillment (B2B warehouse stock), IOSS doesn't apply — goods enter under standard import procedures with Article 23 deferral. The IOSS scheme is designed for B2C shipments under €150 shipped directly from China to the end consumer. Over €150, standard import procedures apply and the full duty-plus-BTW calculation kicks in at the border.

For direct B2C from China to Dutch consumers, IOSS works the same as in any other EU country: register in one EU state (the Netherlands is a common choice), charge Dutch BTW at the point of sale, remit monthly through the IOSS portal, and goods clear BTW-free at the border. The customer sees the all-in price at checkout and gets a clean delivery without a surprise BTW bill from PostNL or DHL. Without IOSS, the carrier collects 21% BTW plus a handling fee (€10–20) from the consumer at delivery — which leads straight to refused packages. Dutch Customs Clearance — AGS System

The Netherlands runs customs clearance through AGS (Aangiftesysteem), the electronic declaration platform. Most freight forwarders in Rotterdam are directly connected to AGS and can file declarations within minutes of vessel arrival. Dutch customs is known for being business-friendly and efficient — one of the reasons Rotterdam dominates EU-China trade. Clearance at Rotterdam for standard container entries typically takes 1–2 days. Air freight through Schiphol clears within hours for express consignments. Physical inspections are selective and risk-based, not blanket — the Dutch customs culture is pragmatic. They want cargo moving, not sitting.

You'll need an EORI number — apply through the Belastingdienst/Douane portal, it's free and typically issued within a week. Dutch customs brokers are competitive: brokerage fees for a standard sea freight entry run €75–150, well below what you'd pay in some other EU member states. For air freight at Schiphol, expect €35–75 per entry. The Netherlands also offers simplified customs procedures for frequent importers: the monthly aggregated declaration (globale aangifte) lets you batch-clear shipments and settle duties monthly rather than per-shipment, further reducing administrative overhead for high-volume importers.

Key Dutch TARIC Codes for Chinese Distribution

The duty rate depends entirely on your HS code. Here are the major chapters and rate ranges for Chinese goods entering through the Netherlands:

  • Chapter 85 (electronics): 0–3.5%. ITA-eligible electronics at 0%, consumer electronics at 2–3.5%. Smartphones (8517 12), laptops (8471 30), and semiconductor products dominate this chapter in Rotterdam's throughput. The Information Technology Agreement eliminates duty on a wide range of electronics — check whether your product qualifies.
  • Chapter 84 (machinery): 0–2.7%. Industrial machinery, mechanical appliances, and parts. Generally lower rates than consumer goods. Many machine tools and industrial components fall near 0–1.7%.
  • Chapters 61–62 (apparel): 5–12%. Knitted and woven garments. Textiles are consistently the highest-rate general cargo category for Chinese imports. Cotton knits typically land at the upper end; synthetic wovens at the lower end.
  • Chapter 94 (furniture): 0–5.6%. Household, office, and institutional furniture. A major category for Dutch distribution centers serving the broader EU market. Wooden furniture (9403 60) and lighting fixtures (9405 40) are the highest-volume subcategories.

For the Netherlands specifically, note that cut flowers and plants (Chapter 06) are a huge Dutch re-export category — though not China-origin dominant, the infrastructure is world-class. The Dutch are the EU's largest re-exporters: goods that enter through Rotterdam are often sold to German, French, or Polish customers after Dutch customs clearance. Dutch BTW Registration for Non-EU Importers

If you hold stock in a Dutch warehouse or fulfillment center, you need a Dutch BTW number — regardless of where your company is based. This is mandatory, not optional. Register through the Belastingdienst (Dutch Tax and Customs Administration). Filing frequency depends on your import volume: monthly for larger operations, quarterly for smaller ones. The Article 23 license requires a separate application — your customs broker can help with the paperwork. Standard processing time for initial BTW registration is 4–8 weeks, so plan ahead before your first shipment arrives at Rotterdam.

You'll need a fiscal representative if your company is non-EU based — this can be your customs broker, your accountant, or a specialized fiscal representative firm. Many non-EU companies use a Dutch BV (besloten vennootschap, or limited company) or a branch office for this purpose. Setup costs and annual compliance typically run €3,000–8,000/year depending on transaction volume and complexity. A Dutch BV can be established in 1–2 weeks through a formation agent, and the minimum share capital requirement was abolished in 2012, so you can incorporate with as little as €1 in equity. Be aware that a BV triggers corporate income tax filing obligations (vpb) in addition to BTW returns, so budget for both.

Product Compliance for the Dutch Market

The Netherlands enforces EU product safety rules through the NVWA (Nederlandse Voedsel- en Warenautoriteit). Standard EU requirements apply: CE marking for electronics and machinery, REACH chemical compliance, and RoHS for electrical and electronic equipment. The EMC Directive (2014/30/EU) and Low Voltage Directive (2014/35/EU) are the two most commonly enforced directives for consumer electronics entering through Rotterdam. For toys, EN 71 testing and CE marking are mandatory.

Dutch packaging rules: companies placing packaged goods on the Dutch market must register with Verpact (formerly Afvalfonds Verpakkingen), the producer responsibility organization for packaging waste. Registration triggers an annual packaging levy based on the weight and material type of packaging you place on the Dutch market. WEEE registration is mandatory for electronics importers through Stichting OPEN. The Dutch are particularly active on sustainability enforcement — expect scrutiny on packaging composition claims and recyclability statements. The NVWA has been ramping up e-commerce import inspections since 2025, particularly on consumer electronics and toys entering through Rotterdam and Schiphol. Non-compliant goods can be detained at the border, re-exported, or destroyed at the importer's expense.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why ship Chinese goods through the Netherlands instead of directly to Germany or France?

Rotterdam's infrastructure, Article 23 VAT deferral, competitive brokerage fees (€75–150 per entry vs. €150–300 in some other EU states), and rapid 1–2 day clearance make it the most cost-effective EU entry point. Once goods clear Dutch customs, they move freely to any EU country — so a shipment destined for German or French customers can enter through Rotterdam, clear once, and be trucked to its final destination without additional customs formalities. The Netherlands has also invested heavily in port automation and digital customs infrastructure: Rotterdam's ECT Delta terminal is among the most automated in the world, and the Port Community System (Portbase) integrates all logistics parties onto a single data platform. Dutch logistics providers offer integrated warehousing, pick-and-pack, and last-mile delivery across the EU, making the Netherlands the default EU market entry point for Chinese exporters at scale. For specific destination-country guides, see our Germany and France calculators.

What's Article 23 and who benefits from it?

Article 23 (douaneregeling 42) allows importers with a Dutch BTW number to defer import VAT from the border to their periodic VAT return. Instead of paying 21% BTW at customs and waiting for a refund, you declare the import BTW as both input and output on the same return — net cash-flow impact is zero for VAT-registered businesses. It's particularly valuable for fulfillment-center operations where goods may sit in warehouse stock for weeks or months before being sold. Without Article 23, a €100,000 shipment at 21% BTW means fronting €21,000+ at the border. With it, that cash stays in your business. The main beneficiaries are non-EU e-commerce sellers using Dutch 3PLs (third-party logistics providers), Amazon FBA sellers with inventory in Dutch fulfillment centers, and any importer holding stock in the Netherlands for EU-wide distribution.

What's the duty rate for Chinese goods entering through Rotterdam?

The average TARIC rate is 3.5% for general Chinese cargo. Actual rates depend entirely on your HS code: ITA-eligible electronics at 0%, consumer electronics at 2–3.5%, machinery at 0–2.7%, apparel at 5–12%, and furniture at 0–5.6%. The 3.5% figure is a realistic blended estimate for mixed-container shipments. Some subcategories are even higher: certain footwear (Chapter 64) can reach 8–17%. Always check your specific TARIC code in the EU TARIC database before quoting a landed cost, and watch for anti-dumping duties which can layer 20–70%+ on top of the base rate for specific products like steel fasteners, aluminium extrusions, and certain ceramics.

Do I need a Dutch company to use Article 23?

No, you don't need a Dutch company, but you do need a Dutch BTW registration and a fiscal representative if your company is non-EU based. Many non-EU companies use a Dutch BV (limited company) or a branch office. Setup costs and annual compliance run €3,000–8,000/year depending on transaction volume. The Article 23 license is a separate application from BTW registration — your customs broker can coordinate both. Standard processing time is 4–8 weeks for initial BTW registration plus 2–4 weeks for the Article 23 license. If you're a non-EU company using a Dutch fulfillment center, your 3PL can often recommend a fiscal representative and broker who handle the entire setup as a package — ask about this before you sign the warehouse contract.
