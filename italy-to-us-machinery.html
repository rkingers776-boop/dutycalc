US Import Duty on Italian Machinery — Low Rates for High-Value Equipment

Italian industrial machinery is precision equipment built in clusters that have specialized for generations. The packaging machinery capital of the world is Bologna — Marchesini, IMA, and Coesia group companies ship billions of dollars of filling lines, blister packers, and cartoners to US pharmaceutical and food plants every year.

Textile machinery clusters in Biella and Prato produce finishing equipment that no other country can match at the same quality tier. Food processing machinery runs through Parma and Modena — pasta extruders, cheese vats, tomato processing lines. Pumps and valves concentrate in Vicenza and Brescia, where hundreds of family-owned foundries produce industrial fluid-handling equipment for refineries, chemical plants, and water treatment facilities worldwide. Woodworking machinery from Rimini — SCM Group and Biesse — dominates the global market for panel saws, edgebanders, and CNC machining centers.

All of this equipment enters the US under standard MFN rates averaging 3.8%. Some subcategories are zero-rated outright — certain industrial robots, specific machine-tool accessories. Others climb to 3.5%, but almost nothing in the Italian machinery export basket exceeds that. There is no Section 301 surcharge. There is no anti-dumping order on Italian machinery categories. There is no additional tariff layer of any kind — just the MFN rate, the Merchandise Processing Fee, and the Harbor Maintenance Fee.

Run the numbers on a real purchase. A $100,000 Marchesini blister packaging line from Bologna, shipped via 40-foot container with $5,000 in ocean freight and marine insurance, gives you a CIF value of $105,000. Duty at 3.8% = $3,990. MPF at 0.3464% of entered value = $363.73. HMF at 0.125% of ocean freight = $131.25. Total government charges: $4,484.98 on $105,000 CIF — an effective rate of 4.3%.

For a piece of capital equipment that will run three shifts for twenty years, the duty line is a rounding error. The bigger costs are never the duty — they are shipping, rigging, installation, commissioning, and bringing the machine into OSHA and NFPA 79 compliance for the US factory floor. For the full machinery-duty picture across every origin — including the Section 301 List 1 stack that Italian equipment avoids — our Machinery Import Duty overview covers it.

The $800 De Minimis — Completely Irrelevant for Machinery

Section 321 of the Tariff Act exempts shipments valued at $800 or less per person per day from all duties, taxes, and fees. That threshold works beautifully for e-commerce — an Italian leather wallet shipped DHL to a Manhattan apartment sails through customs with no duty line. It does not work for industrial machinery.

A replacement pump head from a Vicenza manufacturer runs $1,500 minimum. A single valve actuator, crated and shipped, invoices at $2,000 to $4,000. A spare gear set for a Biella textile loom costs more than the de minimis threshold by itself. Even the smallest Italian machinery spare part lands well north of $800. The entire concept of de minimis is structurally irrelevant to the Italy-to-US machinery trade lane. There is no scenario where a piece of Italian production equipment clears customs under Section 321.

For machinery importers: budget the 3.8% MFN duty on every shipment, treat MPF and HMF as fixed overhead, and ignore de minimis entirely. It is not a cost-saving lever for this product category. For the full rules, see our de minimis value guide.

Key HTS Chapters for Italian Machinery

Italian machinery exports cluster in seven HTS chapter ranges. Know your code before the container leaves Genoa:

  • Chapter 8422: Packaging and bottling machinery — dishwashers, filling machines, capping machines, wrapping machines, cartoners, and blister packers. Bologna's biggest export category by value. 0–3.5% MFN. Most standard packaging lines classify at 3.8%. If your machine also labels or codes, check whether a multi-function classification under 8422.30 applies — the rate is the same, but the statistical suffix matters.
  • Chapter 8445–8452: Textile machinery — carding, combing, spinning, weaving, knitting, finishing, and cutting machines. Italy is the world's leading exporter of textile finishing equipment by a wide margin. Biella (wool finishing) and Prato (recycled wool and specialty finishes) manufacturers dominate the global market. 0–3.8% MFN. Many textile machine subcategories are actually zero-rated — check your specific HTS suffix.
  • Chapter 8438: Food and beverage processing machinery — pasta extruders and dryers, bakery ovens and dividers, dairy processing equipment, tomato processing lines, olive oil presses, and commercial espresso machines. Parma and Modena are the manufacturing centers. 0–3.5% MFN. Espresso machines for commercial use typically classify here, not under consumer appliances — the rate can differ.
  • Chapter 8413: Pumps for liquids — centrifugal, rotary, reciprocating, and diaphragm pumps. Vicenza and Brescia alone produce a double-digit percentage of the world's industrial pumps. 0–3.8% MFN. Most standard centrifugal pumps classify at 0–3.8% depending on construction material and flow characteristics. Submersible and dosing pumps may fall under different subclassifications — verify the 10-digit code.
  • Chapter 8465: Woodworking machinery — sawing, planing, milling, molding, sanding, and edge-banding machines. Italian manufacturers (SCM Group, Biesse) are globally dominant in panel processing and solid wood machining. 0–3.8% MFN. CNC machining centers for wood classify here, not under Chapters 8456–8461 (metal-cutting machine tools) — the distinction is the material being worked.
  • Chapter 8479: Special-purpose machinery — industrial robots not elsewhere specified, automated assembly lines, hydraulic and pneumatic systems, and process-specific manufacturing equipment. 0–3.5% MFN. This is the catch-all for equipment that performs a function not described in the preceding chapters. Italian automation integrators like Comau ship extensively under this chapter.
  • Chapter 8481: Valves, taps, cocks, and pressure regulators — Brescia is the global center for industrial valve manufacturing. Ball valves, gate valves, globe valves, check valves, and control valves from hundreds of Brescian manufacturers flow into US refineries, chemical plants, and water systems. 0–3% MFN.

EU Origin — No Section 301, No Additional Tariffs

Italian machinery is EU origin. That single fact is the most important cost advantage Italian exporters hold over their Chinese competitors in the US market. Section 301 tariffs — the 25% surcharge on List 1 machinery — do not apply to EU goods. Not partially. Not at a reduced rate. They simply do not apply.

A Chinese CNC machining center pays 4.2% MFN plus 25% Section 301 for a combined 29.2% tariff. The same machine from Italy pays 3.8% MFN and nothing else — a 26.7 percentage-point gap that rewrites sourcing economics across entire industries.

Section 232 steel and aluminum tariffs — 25% on steel and 10% on aluminum — also do not apply to finished Italian machinery. Section 232 targets raw metal inputs: steel slab, coil, plate, sheet, and aluminum ingot. A finished packaging machine made of steel does not pay Section 232 — it is a finished capital good under Chapter 84, and CBP has been consistent on this point since the 232 tariffs were implemented in 2018.

The one watchpoint is the Airbus-Boeing dispute. The WTO authorized retaliatory tariffs of 15–25% on $7.5 billion of EU goods, live from 2019 through mid-2021. Those tariffs have been suspended since June 2021 under a 5-year truce that holds through mid-2026. Certain Italian machinery categories appeared on the USTR target lists, and the legal authorization never went away — it is suspended, not rescinded. If negotiations break down, the tariffs could snap back with minimal notice.

Italian Machinery and USMCA — The Transatlantic Angle

A practical note for Italian machinery importers who also have Mexican operations. Italian machinery imported into Mexico pays Mexican import duty — which varies by HTS chapter and is generally higher than US MFN rates. Once that machinery is installed in a Mexican factory and begins producing finished goods, those finished goods may be exported to the US under USMCA preferential tariff treatment.

But here is the key distinction: the Italian machinery itself does not get USMCA treatment. It is not a good being exported from Mexico to the US — it is capital equipment, installed in Mexico, that produced the goods being exported. The USMCA rules of origin and tariff preference apply to the finished goods, not to the machinery that made them.

Where the machinery cost does matter is in the USMCA regional value content (RVC) calculation. Under the transaction value method, the RVC percentage determines whether the finished good qualifies for USMCA preference. The cost of the Italian machinery — including the Mexican import duty paid on it — factors into the producer's total cost, which affects the RVC numerator and denominator. This is not a per-shipment customs calculation; it is an accounting exercise at the producer level, verified by CBP during USMCA audits. If you are running Italian machinery in a Mexican plant and shipping finished goods to the US under USMCA, consult a USMCA trade specialist — this gets complicated fast.

Shipping Italian Machinery — Container, Flat-Rack, and RoRo

Standard 40-foot dry containers handle most individual machines up to about 25 metric tons — a packaging line module, a single textile finishing frame, a pump skid, or a woodworking CNC center. Larger Italian equipment — a complete pasta production line, a marble cutting gantry, an injection molding machine above 500 tons — needs flat-rack containers, open-top containers, or full breakbulk shipment. Flat-rack adds a 20–50% freight premium. Breakbulk is priced per cubic meter or metric ton and requires a freight forwarder who actually knows the Italy-to-US project cargo lane.

Italian export ports break down by industrial region. Genoa serves the northwest — Lombardy and Piedmont machinery goes through Genoa's Voltri terminal. La Spezia, just down the Ligurian coast, is the dominant port for Emilia-Romagna manufacturers (packaging machinery from Bologna, food processing equipment from Parma, ceramic tile machinery from Sassuolo). Trieste in the northeast handles Veneto and Friuli exports — pumps from Vicenza, valves from Brescia, and woodworking machinery from the Veneto cluster.

US entry ports: New York/Newark for East Coast delivery (the default for most Italian imports), Savannah for Southeast distribution, Houston for Gulf Coast oil and gas equipment (especially from the Vicenza/Brescia pump and valve cluster), and Long Beach/Los Angeles for West Coast. Transit time from Genoa or La Spezia to New York/Newark runs 12–14 days direct. To Savannah, add 2–3 days. To Houston, 16–19 days. To Long Beach, 28–32 days via the Panama Canal — some Italian exporters route through an East Coast port and intermodal rail to California instead.

Marine insurance is essential on Italian machinery shipments. All-risk coverage runs roughly 0.3–0.5% of CIF value — $300–500 on a $100,000 machine. The ocean carrier's COGSA liability is capped at $500 per package, which is effectively zero for a six-figure machine. Italian machinery exporters typically quote CIF, arranging insurance on your behalf. Verify that the policy is real, the coverage is all-risk, and the insured value matches the invoice value. A CIF quote with fake or underinsured coverage is worse than an EXW quote where you arrange your own insurance.

CIF Valuation — Bill Duty Against the Full Landed Value, Not the Factory Price

A common mistake among first-time machinery importers is budgeting duty against the factory price alone. CBP calculates duty on the transaction value of the goods plus packing costs, selling commissions, assists, and — critically — international freight and insurance. If you negotiate a Marchesini packaging machine at $100,000 EXW Bologna and separately arrange $5,000 in ocean freight and $500 in marine insurance, your entered value is $105,500, and the 3.8% MFN duty applies to that full amount.

The difference between calculating against $100,000 ($2,500) and $105,500 ($2,637.50) is $137.50 — not huge on a single machine, but real money across a year of purchases. If you are comparing landed costs across Italian suppliers, always convert everything to CIF-equivalent: factory price + inland freight to Genoa or La Spezia + ocean freight + marine insurance. That is the number CBP will use, and that is the number you should plug into the calculator at the top of this page.

One more nuance: "assists" — tooling, molds, engineering work, or components that you, the US buyer, provide to the Italian factory for free or at reduced cost — must be added to the transaction value. If you send a $15,000 injection mold to an Italian factory to produce your parts, CBP expects that $15,000 to be declared as part of the entered value, spread across the production run. Many importers miss this line item entirely, and it is a routine audit trigger.

Customs Bonds for Italian Machinery Entries

Every formal entry for Italian machinery requires a customs bond. For one-off shipments, a single-entry bond (SEB) covers that entry alone and typically costs 0.5–0.7% of the bond amount, which must equal at least the total entered value plus duties and fees. For the $105,000 packaging line example, the bond amount would be roughly $108,120, and a single-entry bond would run $540 to $757.

If you import Italian machinery regularly — more than two or three shipments per year — a continuous bond makes more sense. A continuous bond costs roughly $250–600 per year and covers all entries during its 12-month term. The bond amount is set at 10% of your estimated annual duties and fees, with a $50,000 minimum. For a machinery importer paying $50,000 in annual duties, the continuous bond amount would be $5,000, but the minimum kicks it to $50,000. The bond premium is typically 1–3% of the bond amount — so you are looking at roughly $500–1,500 per year. Your customs broker handles the application, but the cost is yours.

Country of Origin — Italy, Not EU

To claim the standard MFN rate, CBP needs the country of origin clearly stated on the commercial invoice. A detail that trips up new importers every month: "EU" is not an acceptable country of origin on a CBP entry. You need the specific member state — Italy. Every article of foreign origin must be marked in a conspicuous, legible, and permanent manner (19 USC 1304). For machinery, that typically means a metal plate, etched marking, or permanent label attached to the machine itself — not just the packaging.

Goods shipped from an Italian warehouse but manufactured in China carry Chinese origin and pay China's rates, including Section 301 if applicable. The shipping country on the bill of lading does not determine the rate — the manufacturing country does. CBP has gotten very good at detecting transshipment: goods manufactured in China, shipped to an Italian warehouse, and re-exported with Italian paperwork claiming EU origin. If the machine was actually made in China, the origin is China, and Section 301 applies regardless of what the Italian intermediary's invoice says. CBP will catch this.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the duty rate for Italian machinery entering the US?

3.8% average MFN. Most industrial machinery categories fall in the 0–3.5% range. Packaging machinery (Chapter 8422) sits at roughly 3.8%. Textile machinery (Chapters 8445–8452) runs 0–3.8%, with many subcategories zero-rated. Pumps (Chapter 8413) and valves (Chapter 8481) run 0–3%. Woodworking machinery (Chapter 8465) sits at 0–3.8%. Food processing machinery (Chapter 8438) runs 0–3.5%. No Section 301 applies — Italian machinery is EU origin and enters at standard MFN rates only. No anti-dumping orders. No countervailing duties. The rate you see in the HTS is the rate you pay.

Is Italian machinery subject to any additional tariffs?

Currently no. The Airbus-Boeing retaliatory tariffs — which did target some Italian machinery lines at 15–25% — are suspended through mid-2026 under the June 2021 US-EU truce. They could snap back if negotiations fail, but as of now they are not being collected. No Section 301 applies (China-only). No Section 232 applies to finished machinery (it targets raw metal inputs, not assembled capital goods). Italian machinery pays the MFN rate plus MPF (0.3464% of entered value, $31.67 min, $614.35 max) and HMF (0.125% of ocean freight) — nothing beyond that.

Do I need special documentation for Italian machinery imports?

The standard entry package: a detailed commercial invoice showing seller, buyer, full product description, HTS code, unit price, and total value; a packing list with weights, dimensions, and crate numbers; the ocean bill of lading or air waybill; the ISF (Importer Security Filing, due 24 hours before loading at the Italian port); and your customs bond.

The manufacturer's declaration of origin must state "Italy" — not "EU." CBP rejects "European Union" as a country of origin; you need the specific member state on the invoice. If the machinery ships with wood crating or pallets — which almost all Italian equipment does — ISPM 15 heat treatment and marking is mandatory. Non-compliant wood packaging gets the entire shipment held until the wood is treated or re-exported. Most reputable Italian exporters know this, but confirm before the container loads.

If the machine has electrical components, have your NRTL certification documentation ready (UL, ETL, CSA, or TUV) — CBP may ask for it at entry, and OSHA will require it before the machine goes into service on a US factory floor.

What about installation and service — does that affect the customs value?

Installation costs and post-importation service charges are not included in the customs value IF they are separately stated on the commercial invoice as distinct line items. CBP separates the transaction value of imported goods from the value of services performed after importation — rigging, installation, commissioning, training, and ongoing maintenance.

But the invoice must clearly bifurcate these amounts. A single lump-sum price for "machinery plus installation, $150,000" means CBP assesses duty on the full $150,000 because they cannot determine what portion is dutiable goods and what is non-dutiable services. Get your Italian exporter to issue an invoice with separate line items: "Packaging Machine Model X: $140,000" and "Installation, Commissioning, and Training Services: $10,000." That saves you 3.8% on the $10,000 service portion — $250 on a single machine, and it adds up across a factory of Italian equipment.

One more nuance: if the Italian exporter sends technicians to the US to perform installation, those technicians need appropriate visa classification — typically B-1 business visitor for short-duration work, or an E-1/E-2 for longer engagements. CBP officers will ask Italian technicians what they are doing in the US. "Installing machinery we sold to this factory" is perfectly legitimate on a B-1. "Working as a contractor for the US buyer" may not be. Make sure technicians carry a letter from the exporter on company letterhead stating purpose and duration.
