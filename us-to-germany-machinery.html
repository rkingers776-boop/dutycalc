German Import Duty on US Machinery — Low TARIC Rates for Industrial Equipment

US industrial machinery exports to Germany face TARIC MFN rates averaging just 1.7% — among the lowest of any trade lane. The US and EU have no free trade agreement, but TARIC rates on industrial machinery are already very low, with most subheadings falling in the 0–2.7% range. This reflects the EU's policy of keeping industrial inputs cheap for European manufacturers, who depend on affordable imported equipment to stay competitive globally.

Real example: a €50,000 US-made CNC machine with €2,000 shipping equals €52,000 CIF value. The duty at 1.7% comes to €884. German VAT at 19% on €52,884 equals €10,047.96. Total import charges: €10,931.96. Landed cost: €62,931.96. Notice that the VAT line is roughly 11× the duty line. For VAT-registered German buyers, the import VAT is fully recoverable through the UStVA — the net cost impact is the €884 duty only.

Another example at the lower end: a €10,000 industrial pump with €1,200 shipping equals €11,200 CIF. Duty at 1.7% = €190.40. EUSt at 19% on €11,390.40 = €2,164.18. Total landed: €13,554.58. Even on a modest pump, the numbers scale predictably — use the calculator above to run your own scenario.

EU Import VAT at 19% — The Real Cost Driver

Germany's Einfuhrumsatzsteuer (EUSt) at 19% is the dominant cost on US machinery imports. It is calculated on CIF value plus duty. For a €100,000 machine with €5,000 shipping, the CIF is €105,000, duty at 1.7% is €1,785, and EUSt at 19% on €106,785 is €20,289.15 — compare that to the €1,785 duty line. The VAT alone is more than the entire duty bill by a factor of 11.

For VAT-registered German companies, import VAT is paid at customs but recovered through the monthly Umsatzsteuer-Voranmeldung (UStVA). The cash-flow impact lasts 30–60 days. For non-EU companies without German VAT registration, the 19% is a permanent cost. This is why US machinery companies selling into Germany often route sales through a German subsidiary or VAT-registered branch — to recover the 19%.

US exporters who regularly ship to Germany should evaluate whether the volume justifies obtaining a German VAT registration. A fiscal representative (Steuerlicher Vertreter) can handle the registration and monthly filings without requiring a physical German office. The compliance cost must be weighed against the 19% VAT that would otherwise be permanently lost on each shipment.

The €150 Threshold — Completely Irrelevant for Machinery

The EU waives customs duty only below €150; VAT (Einfuhrumsatzsteuer) is charged from the first cent on every import. For a €50,000 CNC machine or even a €10,000 industrial pump, this threshold is laughably irrelevant — every US machinery shipment to Germany goes through full formal customs entry. The €150 line serves low-value e-commerce parcels, not industrial equipment.

There is no scenario where machinery shipped from the US to Germany falls under the €150 threshold — even spare parts for machinery typically exceed €150. Budget for the full duty and VAT on the entire CIF value. The only relevant "relief" for machinery importers is the VAT recovery mechanism for German VAT-registered entities, covered above. Key TARIC Chapters for US Machinery Exports to Germany

Duty rates vary by TARIC subheading. The chapters below cover the vast majority of US machinery exports to Germany. Rates shown are the typical MFN range — always verify your specific subheading against the EU TARIC database before filing your customs declaration.

  • Chapter 8456–8466: Machine tools — 0–2.7%. Covers US CNC equipment from Haas Automation, Hurco, and others. Drilling, milling, turning, and grinding machines.
  • Chapter 8479: Special-purpose machinery — 0–2.7%. Industrial automation, robotics, assembly systems, and custom manufacturing equipment.
  • Chapter 8480–8483: Transmission components — 0–2.7%. Bearings, gears, shafts, and gearboxes. Major US exporters include Timken and Rexnord.
  • Chapter 8501–8504: Electric motors and generators — 0–2.7%. Industrial motors from Baldor, Marathon, and GE.
  • Chapter 8413–8414: Pumps and compressors — 0–2.2%. Gardner Denver, Ingersoll Rand, and other US pump manufacturers.
  • Chapter 9026–9031: Measuring and testing instruments — 0–2.5%. A substantial category for US-made precision instruments, gauges, and calibration equipment from companies like Keysight, Tektronix, and Fluke.
  • Chapter 8426–8429: Heavy construction and earth-moving machinery — 0–2.7%. Bulldozers, excavators, cranes, and road-building equipment from Caterpillar, John Deere, and Terex.
  • Chapter 8431: Parts and attachments for heavy machinery — 0–2.5%. Replacement parts, buckets, blades, and hydraulic components. A high-volume category for US aftermarket parts exporters.

US-EU Trade and the Missing FTA

The US and EU have no comprehensive free trade agreement. TTIP (Transatlantic Trade and Investment Partnership) was negotiated extensively from 2013 to 2016 but was never concluded. For US machinery exporters, this means TARIC MFN rates apply rather than preferential zero-rates that would come with an FTA. The rates are low — averaging 1.7% — so the absence of an FTA costs less than most exporters assume.

The bigger friction for US machinery entering Germany is regulatory compliance — CE marking under the EU Machinery Directive, German safety standards (DGUV, DIN, VDE), and technical documentation — not the tariff. Tariff at 1.7% on a €50,000 machine equals €884. The cost of CE certification and compliance documentation for that same machine might run €5,000–15,000. Compliance is the real cost driver, not duty.

The US runs a trade surplus in machinery with Germany — American machine tools, construction equipment, and industrial automation systems are highly regarded in the German market. The tariff at 1.7% does not meaningfully affect competitiveness. German buyers choose American machinery for its quality and capability, and the duty line is a rounding error in the total procurement decision. See also: Italy to US Machinery and EU to US Duty.

CE Marking and the EU Machinery Directive for US Exporters

US machinery sold into Germany must carry CE marking under the EU Machinery Directive (2006/42/EC), which will be replaced by the new Machinery Regulation (EU 2023/1230) effective January 2027. The manufacturer — or the EU importer acting as the manufacturer's authorized representative — must conduct a risk assessment, compile a technical file, issue a Declaration of Conformity, and affix the CE mark to the equipment.

The new Machinery Regulation introduces stricter requirements including mandatory third-party conformity assessment for certain high-risk machinery categories, digital documentation requirements, and expanded definitions of "machinery" that may capture equipment previously outside the scope. US exporters should review the new regulation now, even though enforcement begins in 2027, because machinery shipped without compliant documentation after the deadline will be refused entry.

US manufacturers without a physical EU presence must appoint an authorized representative based in the EU. This representative takes legal responsibility for the product's compliance within the EU single market. This is separate from customs clearance — German Zoll authorities check for CE marking at the border and may request the Declaration of Conformity before releasing the shipment.

Shipping US Machinery to Germany

Major US export ports for machinery: Houston (Gulf Coast heavy equipment), Charleston and Savannah (Southeast manufacturing), and New York/Newark (Northeast industrial). German entry ports: Hamburg (the primary port for industrial machinery and containerized freight) and Bremerhaven (specialized in RoRo and heavy-lift). Transit time: 12–16 days from the US East Coast to Hamburg. For oversized machinery exceeding standard container dimensions, flat-rack containers or breakbulk shipping are the standard methods.

ISPM 15 wood packaging treatment is mandatory for all wooden crates, pallets, and dunnage entering the EU. Untreated wood will be rejected at the German port, causing delays and re-export costs. Work with a freight forwarder experienced in US-to-Germany machinery moves — they will handle the phytosanitary documentation and coordinate with the receiving agent at Hamburg or Bremerhaven.

Marine insurance: US exporters typically quote FOB or EXW, but German buyers prefer CIF. CIF gives the German buyer certainty on the landed cost at the German port. If you quote EXW, ensure the buyer understands the full freight, insurance, duty, and VAT calculation. A typical insurance premium for machinery shipments is 0.3–0.5% of the cargo value. Customs Clearance and Documentation for US Machinery

Every US machinery shipment entering Germany requires a formal customs declaration (Zollanmeldung) supported by: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, and the CE Declaration of Conformity. Your German customs broker will file the declaration electronically via the ATLAS system. German customs (Zoll) may also request: the technical file, proof of origin (for statistical purposes, not preferential treatment — there is no FTA preference to claim), and ISPM 15 fumigation certificate for wood packaging.

Most US machinery exporters work with a German customs broker (Zollagent) who handles the entry, calculates the duty and EUSt, arranges payment, and coordinates with the freight forwarder for final delivery. Broker fees for a standard machinery entry typically run €150–€400 depending on complexity. The broker will need your EORI number if you are the importer of record — US companies can obtain an EORI from the German customs authority without having a German entity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the most common questions from US machinery exporters shipping to Germany for the first time. For binding advice on your specific situation, consult a German customs broker or a Zollberater familiar with US-origin industrial equipment.

What's the duty rate for US machinery exported to Germany?

The average TARIC MFN rate is 1.7%. Most machine tools, pumps, and industrial equipment fall within the 0–2.7% range. The US and EU have no FTA, but MFN rates on industrial machinery are already very low — the absence of a trade agreement adds minimal cost at the tariff line. To find your exact rate, look up the specific TARIC code for your machinery on the EU TARIC database or ask your German customs broker for a verbindliche Zolltarifauskunft (binding tariff information).

Do I need CE marking to export machinery from the US to Germany?

Yes. The EU Machinery Directive (soon to be the Machinery Regulation) requires CE marking on virtually all industrial machinery. US manufacturers without an EU office need an authorized representative based in the EU. German Zoll can and does check CE compliance at the border — missing documentation will delay or block clearance. Start the CE certification process early; the technical file compilation and notified body review (if required for your machine category) can take 3–6 months for complex equipment.

Is US machinery subject to any additional EU tariffs?

Currently no. The Airbus-Boeing retaliatory tariffs are suspended through mid-2026. There is no Section 301-style equivalent targeting US goods entering the EU. The standard TARIC MFN rates apply without surcharges. That said, trade policy can shift quickly — check the current EU tariff suspension status with your freight forwarder or on the European Commission's trade portal before shipping high-value consignments.

How does German import VAT work for US machinery exporters?

Germany charges 19% EUSt (Einfuhrumsatzsteuer) on CIF value plus duty. German VAT-registered buyers recover the full amount through their monthly Umsatzsteuer-Voranmeldung — it is a cash-flow item, not a permanent cost. Non-VAT-registered importers pay the 19% as a permanent cost with no recovery mechanism.

US companies exporting machinery to Germany should consider obtaining a German VAT registration via a fiscal representative (Steuerlicher Vertreter) if annual export volumes justify the compliance overhead. The registration process typically takes 4–8 weeks. Once registered, you charge German VAT on your invoices, file monthly or quarterly UStVA returns, and recover the import VAT you paid at the border — converting it from a permanent cost to a timing difference.

Duty Deferment and Payment Options

German customs offers several payment mechanisms for regular importers. The most common is the Aufschubkonto (deferred payment account), which allows importers to defer duty and VAT payments rather than paying at the point of entry. To qualify, you need a German EORI number and must post a security deposit with the Hauptzollamt. The deferred amounts are settled monthly — this dramatically reduces the per-shipment administrative burden and smooths cash flow for companies importing regularly.

For one-off or occasional shipments, your customs broker can pay the duty and VAT on your behalf and invoice you — standard practice in the freight forwarding industry. Expect the broker to require payment before releasing the shipment from the port, or to extend credit if you have an established relationship.
