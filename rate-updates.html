Why a rate history page exists

A duty rate is not a property of a product. It is a number a government can move — through a Section 301 review, an anti-dumping sunset, a free trade agreement phase-in schedule, or a legislature arguing about the de minimis line. When the number moves, every landed-cost figure computed before it moved is wrong, and the failure is quiet: the calculator still runs, it just returns last month's answer.

Every lane on this site therefore stores three things that a tariff lookup normally discards: the date its rate was last checked against an official schedule, the source it was checked against, and a flag for lanes whose underlying policy is unsettled. This page is that record in full — 38 entries, every source, every date. Nothing here is generated at runtime; the tables you are reading are the database itself.

What follows is the database as it actually stands, including the lanes nobody has re-checked recently. The bulk of the site was swept on 15 July 2026 and the Gulf lanes were added on 14 September 2026. A two-month-old date is more useful to you than the impression that everything was confirmed this morning.

Where the numbers come from

Each entry's own source string is in the status table above. The list below groups the same authorities by the body that publishes the schedule.

  • United States (17 lanes): USITC Harmonized Tariff Schedule 2026, Federal Register notices, USTR Section 301 exclusion lists (June 2026), CBP CSMS messages, Department of Commerce AD/CVD orders, USMCA Annex 4-B, WTO ITA Annex A and B
  • United Kingdom (5 lanes): UK Global Tariff 2026, HMRC notices including Notice 143, the UK Trade Tariff service
  • European Union (Germany, France, Netherlands, Italy): EU TARIC 2026, national customs authorities (German Zoll, French Douane, Dutch Douane, Agenzia Dogane)
  • Canada (2 lanes): Canada Customs Tariff 2026, CBSA D-Memorandum D8-2-2
  • Mexico: TIGIE 2026, Mexico SAT publications
  • Australia (3 lanes): Australian Customs Tariff 2026, ABF notices, AUSFTA and UK-Australia FTA schedules
  • Japan: Japan Customs Tariff 2026, RCEP implementation schedule
  • South Korea: KORUS FTA schedule, USITC HTS
  • Saudi Arabia and the UAE (2 lanes): GCC Common External Tariff, ZATCA (Saudi VAT and Customs), UAE Federal Tax Authority
  • Global reference entries: WTO World Tariff Profiles 2025, UNCTAD TRAINS database, national customs authorities

How often lanes are checked

  • Lanes with live policy movement (China → US, China → Mexico, the global de minimis reference): checked monthly, and immediately after a published change
  • FTA lanes and stable MFN routes: checked quarterly
  • Long-standing zero-rate FTA lanes: checked every six months
  • Any lane: re-checked within 48 hours of a major published change to its schedule

The dates in the tables above are what actually happened, including where a scheduled pass slipped. If you need a lane re-checked before its next pass, the corridor page names the schedule the number was built from — that is the document to quote if a clearance bill disagrees with our figure.

What "verified" does not mean

Verified means the rate was confirmed against the official schedule on the stated date. It does not mean:

  • The rate applies to your specific product — HS classification decides that, and classification disputes are the most common reason a computed figure and a customs bill diverge
  • The rate is a binding ruling — only the importing country's customs authority can issue one
  • Duty is the whole bill — brokerage, merchandise processing fees, harbour maintenance fees and inspection costs sit on top of the rate in this table

Check the figure with your customs broker before you file entry paperwork. DutyCalc hands you an estimate with a date and a source attached; your broker hands you the filing.
