Auto Parts Import Duty: It Depends on the Metal, Not the Brand

Two brake rotors land at the Port of Long Beach on the same ship. The one boxed in Aichi Prefecture owes 2.5%. The one boxed in Shandong owes 27.5%. Same part, same function, same container — and a 25-point swing that has nothing to do with quality and everything to do with origin. Here's how the map actually works.

The one mistake every first-time auto parts importer makes
Lumping everything under "Chapter 87 auto parts" on the invoice. CBP wants each part on its own HTS line — brake calipers under 8708.30, ECUs under 8543.70, gaskets under 8484.10. A consolidated "auto parts" line is a guaranteed manual review. Run the HS Code Estimator before you submit the 7501.

Why "auto parts duty" is a range, not a rate

The auto parts trade doesn't fit in one tariff number. It spans at least four HTS chapters, three regulatory agencies, and a rate spread from 0% to over 40% once anti-dumping deposits are factored in. The single biggest variable isn't what the part is — it's where it was made. A Denso alternator made in Japan owes 2.5%. A Denso alternator made in China owes 27.5%. The brand and the SKU are identical; the country of origin isn't.

This page walks through duty by part type, by origin country, and by the extra layers most calculators don't show. It won't replace a binding ruling, but it'll tell you which questions to ask before you wire the supplier.

The four HTS chapters every auto parts importer hits

  • Chapter 8708 — Parts and accessories of motor vehicles. The workhorse. Brake pads, rotors, calipers (8708.30); suspension arms, struts, shocks (8708.80); body panels, bumpers, fenders (8708.10); radiators (8708.91); exhaust systems (8708.92); clutches (8708.93); steering wheels and columns (8708.94). Most lines carry 2.5% MFN.
  • Chapter 8483 — Transmission shafts, gears, bearings, housed units. Gearboxes, differentials, ball screws, roller bearings, oil seals, universal joints. 2.5% MFN on most, but bearings from China carry anti-dumping deposits that can push the effective rate past 40%.
  • Chapter 8409/8413 — Engine parts and pumps. Pistons, cylinder liners, intake/exhaust valves, fuel injection nozzles, oil pumps, water pumps, turbocharger cartridges. Mostly 2.5% MFN. Turbochargers (8414.80) sit in a separate chapter but follow the same rate structure.
  • Chapter 8511 — Electrical ignition and starting. Spark plugs, ignition coils, starter motors, alternators, distributors. 2.5% MFN across the board. NGK, Denso, and Bosch dominate this chapter across all origins.

The first six digits of any of these codes are globally harmonized. Digits seven through ten are US-specific, and they're where your actual rate is decided. A brake caliper (8708.30) and a suspension control arm (8708.80) are both "auto parts" but live on different CBP 7501 lines with different subheading-level exclusion histories. Your broker needs the 10-digit code, not the chapter number.

Duty by part type — the spread is wider than most people think

The rates below are representative of current US HTS subheadings for the most commonly imported categories. They're accurate enough to budget with but not a substitute for a broker's classification.

Brakes, rotors, pads, calipers: 2.5% base — but check the origin

Brake components (8708.30) run 2.5% MFN for all non-China origins. The catch: Chinese brake rotors and pads have been hit with anti-dumping orders dating back to at least 2015, with some dumping margins calculated in the 40–90% range for non-cooperating factories. A $5,000 shipment of Chinese brake rotors at 2.5% should owe $125 in duty — but if the factory is on the AD list, CBP collects a deposit at the published margin, and the effective rate can exceed the invoice value of the parts. Ask the supplier for their AD case number before you quote a landed cost. If they don't know it, that's your answer.

Engine parts: stable and predictable

Pistons, rings, cylinder liners, valves, and fuel injectors (8409) universally run 2.5% MFN regardless of origin. The market is dominated by Japanese and German OEM suppliers (Denso, Bosch, Mahle, Federal-Mogul) who ship from non-China facilities, so Section 301 rarely applies. For China-origin engine parts, the base 2.5% plus 25% Section 301 List 1 gives 27.5% effective — which is why most engine components for the US aftermarket come from Japan, Korea, Germany, or Mexico, not China. The Section 301 math alone has reshaped the supply base.

Transmissions, gearboxes, bearings: 2.5% — with a bearing-shaped asterisk

Transmission assemblies, gear shafts, differentials (8483) all run 2.5% MFN. Tapered roller bearings, ball bearings, and housed bearing units from China carry anti-dumping orders at published margins of 0–92% depending on the exporter (NTN, NSK, and Timken Chinese subsidiaries have their own rates). If you're importing Chinese-made bearings, the AD deposit is handled at entry as a cash deposit to CBP — it's not a duty, it's an estimated penalty against future dumping, but functionally it adds to your landed cost up front. The AD reviews take years; the deposit is refundable (with interest) if the final rate is lower, but most importers treat it as a cost and move on.

ECUs, sensors, wiring: surprisingly mild on duty, heavy on compliance

Engine control units fall under 8543.70 (2.5% MFN) or 9032.89 (0–2.5% depending on function). Oxygen sensors, MAF sensors, and ABS wheel-speed sensors typically fall under 9031 or 9029 at 0–2.5%. The regulatory lift — EPA tampering rules, CARB EO numbers for California, FMVSS compliance — is often the bigger cost than the duty line. A $200 replacement ECU that pays $5 in duty but requires a $15,000 CARB test for a new EO number is not really a $205 product.

Tires: 3.4–4%, and it matters at volume

New pneumatic tires (4011) run 3.4% for passenger radials and up to 4% for light truck and specialty tires depending on construction and size. Chinese tires face an additional anti-dumping and countervailing duty landscape that has shifted repeatedly since 2015, with current composite AD/CVD rates ranging from 10–45% by manufacturer. Passenger tires from Japan, Korea, and Germany pay the MFN rate only. Tire importers live in an entirely separate compliance ecosystem: DOT marking (FMVSS 109/119/139), treadwear/UTQG labeling, and the NHTSA registration database. The import process resembles general auto parts but the regulatory layer is thicker.

Duty by origin country — the same part, six different outcomes

This is the section where most margin models break. The base MFN rate on auto parts is a narrow band (2.5% for most, 3.4–4% for tires). The surcharges and exclusions are where the real spread lives.

  • From Japan: 2.5% MFN on virtually all auto parts. No Section 301. No broad anti-dumping orders (specific bearing factories have AD orders, but most Japanese exporters are not covered). The US-Japan Trade Agreement does not reduce auto parts duties below MFN — the rate was already low and the deal focused on agricultural market access instead. Japanese parts are the cost-baseline for the US aftermarket. Calculator: Japan → US Auto Parts.
  • From Germany: 2.5% MFN on all Chapter 8708 categories. No Section 301. No Section 232 on finished assemblies (the steel/aluminum tariffs apply to raw metal articles, not assembled parts). The 2021 US-EU auto truce is holding in 2026, but it's a truce not a treaty — the threatened 25% tariff on European auto exports has not materialized across any administration. Calculator: Germany → US Automotive.
  • From China: 2.5% MFN + 25% Section 301 (List 1) on most auto parts = 27.5% effective. This applies to virtually every Chapter 87, 84, and 85 auto part unless a specific exclusion covers the 10-digit code. Exclusions have been narrow and product-specific — think a single bearing size, not "all bearings" — and many expired without renewal. On top of the Section 301 layer, anti-dumping orders on brake rotors, certain bearings, and aluminum extrusions can push the total beyond 40% for specific products from specific factories. This rate gap is the structural reason Nissan, Honda, and Toyota shifted most parts production for US-market vehicles out of China years ago. See Section 301 Guide for the exclusion list and history.
  • From Korea: Most auto parts enter at 0% under KORUS, which went into effect in 2012 and eliminated auto parts tariffs on an accelerated schedule. The few subheadings that were not fully phased hold at 0–2.5%. For US importers sourcing Korean OEM and aftermarket parts — Hyundai/Kia replacement components, Mando brake systems, Hanon HVAC components — the duty landscape is about as favorable as it gets. No Section 301 and a mature FTA with zero parts rates: it's why Korean parts have been steadily gaining shelf space in the US aftermarket.
  • From Mexico: 0% under USMCA for parts meeting the 75% regional value content threshold. This is the whole story of the Guanajuato automotive cluster — Denso, Aisin, NSK, Bosch, and dozens of Tier-1 suppliers operate Mexican plants whose output qualifies for zero-duty entry. Parts merely transshipped through Mexican warehouses without substantial transformation pay the origin country's rate, not USMCA zero. If your Japanese-made bearings sit in a Laredo warehouse for three weeks before crossing, they're still Japanese origin and still owe 2.5%. For the nearshoring math, see: IMMEX Nearshoring.
  • From the UK: Post-Brexit, UK-origin auto parts enter the US at 2.5% MFN with no additional trade-remedy duties. The US-UK trade agreement is modest and does not reduce industrial tariffs. UK auto parts exporters are a small but specialized niche — mostly high-end engine components, motorsport parts, and classic car restoration pieces — where the 2.5% rate is rarely the deciding factor in a purchase decision.

A note on transshipment that matters more in auto parts than almost any other category: CBP knows the auto parts supply chain intimately. A Chinese brake rotor shipped to Vietnam for "repackaging" does not become Vietnamese origin and does not shed the 25% Section 301. Substantial transformation requires a change in name, character, or use — not a new box. Attempted origin laundering through third-country transshipment has been a CBP enforcement priority in auto parts since 2018 and has resulted in significant penalty actions. If your supplier proposes a "simple transshipment solution" to avoid China tariffs, the correct response is to find a real alternative manufacturing location.

Compliance that stops the shipment — whether or not the duty is right

The 2.5% or 27.5% on your 7501 is the easy math. These are the things that get auto parts held at the port, and none of them show up in a rate lookup tool.

NHTSA and FMVSS: safety-critical parts need paperwork

NHTSA regulates a specific list of replacement parts that must meet Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS): brake hoses, brake fluid, lighting equipment, tires, airbag components, seat belt assemblies, and glazing (windows/windshields). These must bear the DOT symbol and the manufacturer's DOT registration code. The importer of record is responsible for compliance — not the factory, not the forwarder, and not the broker. If CBP flags a shipment of brake hoses with no DOT marking, they'll hold it until you provide a conformity certificate or re-export it. There's no "pay the penalty and keep the goods" option for non-compliant safety parts.

Less obvious but equally enforced: motorcycle helmets (FMVSS 218), child restraint systems (FMVSS 213), and replacement lighting equipment (FMVSS 108). A set of LED headlights from an overseas supplier that doesn't carry the DOT marking is importable as "off-road only," but selling them for on-road use invites liability far beyond the customs issue.

EPA: emissions parts and the "racing use only" exemption

EPA regulates emissions-related replacement parts under the Clean Air Act: catalytic converters, oxygen sensors, ECUs (if they control emissions parameters), evaporative emissions canisters, EGR valves, and PCV valves. New aftermarket catalytic converters must be EPA-certified for the specific vehicle application — universal cats are not certifiable — and must carry an EPA code on the shell. Used catalytic converters cannot be imported except under very narrow EPA exemptions, and CBP has a standing alert on used cat imports.

The "racing use only" exemption for emissions parts is real but narrow. The part must be permanently marked "for racing use only," the importer must maintain documentation supporting the racing designation, and the vehicle must be operated exclusively in organized competition — no street registration, no odometer, no incidental road use. If you're importing "racing ECUs" for a street-tuned Subaru community, the exemption does not apply and the liability sits with the importer.

CARB: California is its own compliance layer

California's Air Resources Board (CARB) maintains an Executive Order (EO) system for emissions-related aftermarket parts sold in California. A part can be entirely EPA-legal and still lack a CARB EO number, making it unsellable in California. For importers distributing nationally, the question is commercial, not customs — CBP does not enforce CARB at the border — but if your biggest customers are in California, you need EO coverage regardless of what CBP says at entry.

The Chicken Tax: 25% on light trucks, still in effect after 63 years

In 1963, Europe taxed US chicken exports. President Johnson retaliated with a 25% tariff on light trucks (HTS 8704), potato starch, dextrin, and brandy, under Presidential Proclamation 3564. The other tariffs eventually came off. The truck tariff didn't. Six decades later, it remains the single most consequential automotive tariff in US trade law, and it has shaped global vehicle manufacturing strategy more than any trade deal since.

Here's what it means for auto parts importers specifically: if you're importing a vehicle classified as a light truck — a cargo van with a flat load floor, no rear seats, and a GVWR under a certain threshold — you're paying 25% on the CIF value, not 2.5%. A $40,000 Mercedes Sprinter cargo van would owe $10,000 in duty, which is precisely why Mercedes builds Sprinters in South Carolina. But the chicken tax doesn't touch auto parts — a Sprinter brake rotor imported on its own (8708.30) pays 2.5% regardless of what vehicle it's destined for. The classification boundary is between complete vehicles and loose parts, and it's one of the few places where keeping your goods disassembled actually saves money.

For importers of light-duty truck parts specifically — pickup and cargo van components — the 25% vehicle tariff means the US replacement market for those vehicles is almost entirely supplied by North American-assembled units, which means the parts are often sourced from USMCA-regional suppliers rather than offshore. That supply-chain fact matters more to your landed cost than the 2.5% duty on the individual part.

De minimis: mostly irrelevant to commercial auto parts

Section 321's $800 de minimis exemption works for a single replacement sensor, a set of spark plugs, or a gasket kit ordered directly from an overseas supplier. The shipment clears with zero customs cost — no duty, no MPF, no HMF. For commercial auto parts distributors bringing in pallets or containers, de minimis is irrelevant: a crate of 200 brake rotors is well over $800, and the full 2.5% (or 27.5%, or the AD rate) applies to the entire CIF value. There's no incremental exemption — it's all-or-nothing at $800 per person per day.

One pattern worth flagging: a US repair shop ordering from three different Japanese suppliers on three separate invoices, each under $800, each arriving on different days, receives three duty-free shipments. That's Section 321 operating as designed. But if the same shop places one $2,400 order that the supplier artificially splits into three $800 shipments on three consecutive days to stay under the threshold, CBP considers that a structured transaction and may consolidate the shipments for duty assessment. The de minimis rules are increasingly enforced; read the 2026 Section 321 reform for the latest on enforcement trends and the narrowed de minimis scope for certain Chinese-origin goods.

How to estimate your actual landed cost

The workflow, in order of importance:

  1. Nail the 10-digit HTS code per SKU. Not per shipment — per part number. A brake caliper and a brake pad are different subheadings. A turbocharger and an oil pump are in different chapters. Your broker can classify based on your part descriptions, but the more accurate the description ("hydraulic disc brake caliper, cast iron, for passenger vehicle" rather than "brake part"), the less chance of a post-entry reclassification and penalty.
  2. Check the origin — not the shipping port. The country of substantial transformation decides Section 301 and AD applicability. A part manufactured in China and warehoused in Japan is still Chinese origin. Get the factory's physical manufacturing address, not the freight forwarder's consolidation warehouse.
  3. Look up any anti-dumping or countervailing duty orders. This applies mostly to China-origin brake rotors, certain bearings, aluminum extrusions, and steel-intensive parts. The International Trade Administration's AD/CVD search portal is public; search by country and product before you commit to the purchase order. A 40% AD deposit changes the economics of the entire shipment.
  4. Apply the duty rate to CIF. CBP uses CIF value (goods + insurance + freight) not your FOB factory price. Sea freight from Yokohama to LA/LB might add 3–6% to the goods value on a per-unit basis at container scale; air freight from Frankfurt for a rush order can add 15–25%. The duty is calculated on the larger number.
  5. Add MPF and HMF. Merchandise Processing Fee: 0.3464% of entered value, minimum $31.67, maximum $614.35 (formal entry). Harbor Maintenance Fee: 0.125% of entered value, ocean freight only. These sound small but on a $100,000 parts container they add roughly $470 — about half a percent — to the total.

For a fast estimate, use the corridor-specific calculators on the Japan → US and Germany → US pages. The Landed Cost Calculator handles the CIF + duty + fees math once you supply the rate. Documentation checklist

Your customs broker needs every line on this list. Missing one item delays the shipment; missing two gets it flagged:

  • Commercial Invoice — itemized by part number with HTS code per line, country of origin per line, unit value, and total value. "Various auto parts" is not a valid description.
  • Packing List — box-level weights and dimensions. Critical for air freight consolidation where a single airway bill covers dozens of part numbers.
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) / Airway Bill (air) — carrier-issued transport document. Japanese parts typically move via air freight from Narita/Haneda; German parts via FRA; Chinese parts via Shanghai/Ningbo/Shenzhen, largely by sea.
  • CBP Form 7501 — entry summary, filed by your broker. 10-digit HTS codes required per line. Your broker cannot guess; supply the codes.
  • Importer Security Filing (ISF-10) — ocean shipments only, 24 hours before vessel loading at origin. Your forwarder or broker files it; you supply the underlying data.
  • DOT/NHTSA registration — if importing safety-regulated parts (brake hoses, lighting, glazing, tires). The manufacturer must be registered with NHTSA and the parts must bear the DOT symbol. For tires, the NHTSA tire identification number (TIN) is required on the sidewall.

For China-origin parts specifically, keep a copy of the manufacturer's Section 301 exclusion certificate (if one exists for your HTS code) and the factory's anti-dumping case number (if applicable). CBP's ACE system cross-references these at entry; a missing exclusion claim means the full 25% Section 301 rate applies by default. You can claim an exclusion retroactively through a Post-Summary Correction, but it's a paper-intensive process and the refund takes months.

Frequently asked questions

What's the duty rate for auto parts imported to the US?

2.5% MFN for most parts — brake components, engine internals, transmission parts, suspension systems, ignition/electrical, and body panels all cluster around this rate. Tires (Chapter 4011) run 3.4–4%. The rate is the same for OEM and aftermarket parts of the same type — CBP classifies by what the part is, not who made it or which brand is on the box. China-origin parts face an additional 25% Section 301 surcharge (List 1) unless a specific exclusion applies, bringing the effective rate to 27.5%.

Why do Chinese brake rotors cost so much to import?

Three layers: 2.5% MFN base + 25% Section 301 List 1 + anti-dumping deposits that vary by factory (some published margins exceed 40%). The combined effective rate can reach 60–90% for a non-cooperating factory, though most importers source from factories with individually determined lower AD rates. The AD deposit is collected at entry as a cash deposit to CBP and is technically refundable if the final rate in the administrative review is lower — but reviews take years and most importers treat the deposit as a sunk cost.

Do OEM parts pay different duty than aftermarket parts?

No. A Denso OEM alternator made in Japan and a generic aftermarket alternator made in Japan go under the same HTS subheading at the same 2.5% rate. Brand, OEM certification, and IATF 16949 compliance affect the commercial invoice value — a $400 Denso unit generates $10 in duty vs $5 on a $200 generic unit — but the rate is identical. CBP classifies by the physical article, not the box.

How does the Chicken Tax affect auto parts imports?

It doesn't — it applies to complete light trucks (HTS 8704), not loose parts. A Mercedes Sprinter cargo van pays 25% to import as a vehicle; a Sprinter brake caliper imported as a replacement part pays 2.5%. The chicken tax is why Sprinters are built in South Carolina, but it doesn't touch the parts supply chain. The classification distinction between a complete vehicle and a loose part is well-established and rarely contested.

Can I import auto parts without DOT or EPA certification?

Depends on the part. Brake hoses, lighting, glazing, tires, airbags, and seat belts require DOT compliance and manufacturer registration. Emissions components — catalytic converters, oxygen sensors, ECUs — require EPA certification if sold for on-road use. Non-regulated parts like brake rotors, suspension arms, gaskets, filters, and most mechanical components require no federal certification. But even for non-regulated parts, NHTSA can issue a recall if a defect is discovered post-import, and the importer of record is the party on the hook.

Does the $800 de minimis work for auto parts?

For a single sensor, a set of spark plugs, or a gasket kit — yes. For commercial quantities — no. The $800 threshold is per person per day, all-or-nothing: one shipment at $799 clears free; one shipment at $801 pays duty on the full $801. Auto parts distributors moving pallets or containers should budget the full duty rate. See the de minimis guide for the full rules across all markets.
